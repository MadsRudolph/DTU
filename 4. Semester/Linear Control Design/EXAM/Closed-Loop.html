01

Systems & transfer functions

~50 min

Core ideas

  • A transfer function G(s) = num/den is the input→output law of a linear system. The poles (denominator roots) are the dynamics: each pole −p contributes e−pt.
  • Stable ⇔ every pole strictly in the left half-plane. One RHP pole and the output grows. First thing to check, always.
  • DC gain G(0) = final value of the unit-step response (if stable). Plug s = 0.
  • Block algebra: series multiply, parallel (summed at a junction) add, negative feedback forward/(1 + loop). Reduce inside-out: innermost loop first.
  • ODE → TF with zero initial conditions — but normalise first: collect every y-term on the left before reading coefficients.
  • Linearisation = tangent at an operating point: A = ∂f/∂x, B = ∂f/∂u ⇒ G = B/(s−A). Valid for small deviations only. A linear ODE needs no linearisation.

Second-order response

~60 min

Core ideas

  • Standard form ωₙ²/(s² + 2ζωₙs + ωₙ²): ζ sets the shape (overshoot, ringing), ωₙ sets the speed.
  • Poles at −ζωₙ ± jω_d with ω_d = ωₙ√(1−ζ²) — the ringing you SEE is ω_d, always below ωₙ.
  • Overshoot is measured relative to the final value: M_p = (y_peak − y_ss)/y_ss.
  • Settling: the envelope e−ζωₙt decays at the pole real part; 2 % band ⇒ t_s = 4/(ζωₙ).
  • ζ ≥ 1: no overshoot at all (M_p formula forbidden). Negative ζ: unstable — kill that option on sight.

Watch: raising ωₙ only squeezes time — the SHAPE (overshoot) never changes unless ζ moves. The dashed lines are the ±2 % settling band.

03

Bode plots

~60 min

Core ideas

  • Asymptote rules: each pole bends the slope −20 dB/dec at its corner and adds −90° of phase (−45° AT the corner); each zero does the opposite.
  • dB → linear always first: |G| = 10^(dB/20). A flat low-frequency level IS the DC gain.
  • Integrator signature: phase sits at −90° at low ω and the magnitude is a −20 dB/dec ramp with no flat DC level.
  • RHP zero: magnitude rises +20 dB/dec like a normal zero, but phase DROPS −90° like a pole. Magnitude of a zero, phase of a pole — that mismatch is the non-minimum-phase fingerprint.
  • Final slope = −20·(#poles − #zeros) dB/dec; final phase = −90°·(#poles − #zeros).

04

Stability, margins & Nyquist

~70 min

Core ideas

  • GM lives at the phase crossover ω_π (∠G = −180°): GM = 1/|G(jω_π)| — the gain increase that would hit −1.
  • PM (γ_M) lives at the gain crossover ω_c (|G| = 1): γ_M = 180° + ∠G(jω_c) — the extra lag the loop can absorb. Never swap the two crossovers.
  • Nyquist: Z = N + P. Z = closed-loop RHP poles (want 0), P = open-loop RHP poles, N = clockwise encirclements of −1. Stable plant ⇒ don't encircle; plant with P RHP poles ⇒ need exactly P counter-clockwise encirclements.
  • Gain K scales the whole Nyquist plot radially — and moves no phase, so the crossover for a target PM is pinned by the plant alone.
  • The flip: stable plant → stable for 0 < K < GM. Plant with an RHP pole → stable for K > K_crit. The critical value itself is only marginal.

Steady-state error

~50 min

Core ideas

  • Derive, don't recite: E = R/(1+L); FVT with R = 1/s gives e_ss = 1/(1 + L(0)). That's where the 1+ comes from — dropping it cost you June Q5.
  • System type = poles at the origin in L(s). Type 0: finite step error 1/(1+K_p). Type 1: zero step error, ramp error 1/K_v. Type 2: zero ramp error.
  • An integrator is a pole exactly at s = 0 — a pole at −5 raises nothing.
  • Scale by the input: a step of 30 makes every table entry ×30 (June Q5: 30/1.03 = 29, not 30).
  • Disturbance at the plant input: y_ss = lim Gd/(1+L). Rejected completely only if the integrator sits between the error and the injection (in the controller) — a plant integrator is inside Gd too and cancels.
  • S = 1/(1+L) (ref→error) and T = L/(1+L) (ref→output): S + T = 1 at every frequency — you can't push both down at once.

Controller design

~70 min

Core ideas

  • Division of labour: Lag/PI buys steady-state accuracy; Lead buys phase margin (transients); PI-Lead buys both.
  • The phase budget at the chosen crossover: −180° + γ_M = φ_G + φ_Lead + φ_PI. The plant gives φ_G, the lead pays IN (up to φ_max), the PI part pays OUT (−arctan(1/N_i)). Solve for whichever of α, N_i, K_P is unknown.
  • Lead C_d = (τ_d·s+1)/(α·τ_d·s+1) with α < 1: zero BEFORE pole. Centre at the geometric mean: τ_d = 1/(ω_c√α). It boosts gain 1/√α at centre. The reciprocal is a lag — the trap that cost you two June questions.
  • P-design: phase pins ω_c (∠G = −180°+γ_M), then gain sets the crossover (K_P = 1/|G|).
  • Feed-forward: ideal F = 1/G — but improper if G is strictly proper, and an RHP zero makes 1/G unstable: fall back to the static F(0), which still nails DC.
Convention flag: the exam writes the lead with α < 1 (form above, φ_max = arcsin((1−α)/(1+α))). Your cheat-sheet §8 uses α = p/z > 1 in one place. In the oral, state the form you're using before you use it.

The course's recipe (Lecture 8/9) — say these steps in this order

This exact sequence is what the slides teach as the Phase Margin Design Method. In the oral, narrating THIS order is the difference between "knows formulas" and "can design".

  • 1 · Choose the specs: γ_M (start 60°, range 45–75°), N_i (start 3, range 2–10), α (start ~0.2, down to 0.02 for more phase).
  • 2 · Compute the parts' phases: φ_i = −arctan(1/N_i) and φ_m = arcsin((1−α)/(1+α)).
  • 3 · Phase balance → target plant phase: φ_G = γ_M − φ_i − 180° − φ_m.
  • 4 · Find ω_c on the plant's Bode phase plot where ∠G = φ_G.
  • 5 · Time constants: τ_d = 1/(ω_c√α), τ_i = N_i/ω_c.
  • 6 · Gain condition: K_P = 1/|C_PI·C_d·G|(jω_c) — the loop crosses 0 dB at ω_c.
  • 7 · Check the step response and iterate if needed.
Trade-offs to say out loud: bigger N_i = less phase cost but slower error correction · smaller α = more phase but more high-frequency noise gain · bigger γ_M = more robust but slower.

Practical limits & REGBOT

~40 min

Core ideas (Lectures 2, 10, 11, 12 — the ones cramming usually skips)

  • Ziegler-Nichols (Lec 2): step reference, P-only, raise K until sustained oscillation → note ultimate gain K_u and period P_u → read the controller from the table. It's the no-model tuning method.
  • Integrator windup (Lec 11): when the actuator saturates, the integral keeps accumulating error it can't act on; when the error finally flips, the bloated integral takes ages to discharge → huge overshoot. Anti-windup: feed (u − u_sat)·K_a back to reduce the integrator's input — zero effect when unsaturated, freezes the integrator when saturated.
  • Rate limits (Lec 11): with a rate-limited actuator, place the crossover near the plant's dominant pole to avoid demanding slew the actuator can't deliver.
  • Pre-filter (Lec 11/12): sits OUTSIDE the loop on the reference — smooths setpoint steps and cuts overshoot without touching stability margins (the loop never sees it).
  • Cascaded control (Lec 10, REGBOT): position → velocity → tilt → wheel loops. Design inside-out, each inner loop significantly faster than its outer, so the outer design can treat it as instantaneous.
  • Unstable plants (Lec 10): Nyquist needs one CCW encirclement of −1 per RHP pole — gain must stretch (or with negative gain, flip) the plot past −1. Low ω_c isn't automatically safer: it must outrun the unstable pole.

Traps & exam craft

~40 min

Your five traps from June — now spoken checks

  • 1 · Finish the algebra. The +1 in 1/(1+K_p); the strict inequality (K > K_crit, the critical value is only marginal).
  • 2 · Lead orientation. Zero before pole, gain UP by 1/√α. Reaching for the reciprocal = lag.
  • 3 · Physics first. Negative ζ, ω_d = ωₙ with finite overshoot, "stable" with an RHP pole — kill impossible options out loud before computing.
  • 4 · Read the origin. Phase −90° at low ω = integrator. Low-frequency level = steady-state behaviour.
  • 5 · RHP zero rules. Undershoot, capped bandwidth, static-only feed-forward.

True or trap? — the concept statements

"With relative degree ≥ 3, a large enough gain always destabilises the loop."

Final oral drills — full 2-minute answers

Say it aloud

"Tell me about gain and phase margin." (the most likely opener there is)

  • Both defined AT their crossovers: GM at ω_π (∠G = −180°), PM at ω_c (|G| = 1)
  • GM = 1/|G(jω_π)| (quote in dB); γ_M = 180° + ∠G(jω_c)
  • Physical: tolerance to gain increase / added lag; a delay τ eats ω_c·τ of PM
  • Close with the design rule of thumb γ_M ≈ 100·ζ
Say it aloud

"Why do we use a PI-Lead controller? Walk me through the design idea."

  • PI: integrator ⇒ type +1 ⇒ zero step error — but costs phase
  • Lead: positive phase bump at ω_c ⇒ restores/raises margin
  • Design = balance the phase budget at the chosen ω_c, then set K_P from the magnitude condition
  • Name the trade-offs: N_i (error speed vs phase cost), α (phase vs noise gain)
