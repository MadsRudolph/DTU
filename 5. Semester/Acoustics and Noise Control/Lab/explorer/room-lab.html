Part A · about 30 minutes · sine generator + your ears

The room is a resonator

Between two hard walls the air can't move into the wall, so the pressure peaks there. A tone fits when a whole number of half-wavelengths spans the room.

At a rigid wall the particle velocity u must be zero, so the pressure has a maximum there. Between two walls a distance L apart, a standing wave fits when L = n·λ/2, which gives fn = n·c/(2L). The pressure shape is cos(nπx/L): it has n quiet planes (nodes) between the walls. Watch the air do it:

A box is three of these at once. Every combination of whole numbers (nx, ny, nz) is a mode:

f = (c/2) · √[ (nx/Lx)² + (ny/Ly)² + (nz/Lz)² ]

One non-zero index is an axial mode (bounces between two parallel walls, the strongest), two is tangential, three is oblique. The lab room is 4.38 × 3.13 × 3.29 m. It's taller than it is wide, so the first height mode (0,0,1) comes before the first width mode (0,1,0).

Below, the room is simulated as a sum of its modes, driven from the corner where the lab's loudspeaker stands. Do what you'll do on the day: sweep the generator until the room booms, then drag the listener (the ring) around the floor plan and find the quiet spots. The number of quiet planes along each direction is the mode's index.

Why is the corner such a good place for the speaker and for listening?

Every mode has a pressure maximum at every rigid wall, because cos(nπx/L) = ±1 at x = 0 and x = L. A corner is at a wall in all three directions at once, so every mode has its maximum there. A loudspeaker in a corner drives all modes (it pushes on the pressure), and an ear or a phone in a corner hears all of them. In the middle of the room every mode with an odd index has a node and goes silent. Drag the ring to the centre of the plan in Fig. A2 at 39.2 Hz to hear it vanish.

Why will your measured frequencies be a little off?

The speed of sound changes by about 0.6 m/s per °C, so every mode moves by 0.18 % per kelvin (about 0.1 Hz). The real room isn't a perfect box, the walls and door give a little, and the room's dimensions are "approximate" in the guide. Flexible walls make the room act slightly larger, which lowers the modes. Write down the temperature on the day.

Why only the lowest five?

The number of modes grows roughly with f³. Above the Schroeder frequency fS = 2000·√(T/V) so many overlap that you can no longer pick one out, and the sound field is called diffuse. For this room (V = 45.1 m³) that's roughly 400–950 Hz depending on T. Part A lives far below it; Parts B and C rely on being above it, which is also why the 125 Hz band is the shakiest in Part B.

Part B · about 60 minutes · pink noise + MATLAB

How fast the room forgets

Feed power in and the walls take a fixed fraction out per bounce. Switch the source off and the level falls by the same number of dB every second: a straight line in dB.

Well above the Schroeder frequency the sound energy is spread evenly and arrives from every direction: a diffuse field. Then one number describes the room, the energy density w, and the intensity hitting any wall is w·c/4. Each surface absorbs a fraction α of what hits it, so the room has an absorption area A = Σ Si·αi (m², an "open window" equivalent). The energy balance is

V · dw/dt = W − (c A/4) · w

With the source on, the level settles where input equals loss, w = 4W/(cA). With it off, w decays exponentially, which is a straight line in dB. The time to fall 60 dB is Sabine's reverberation time:

T = 0.161 · V / A   [s, with V in m³ and A in m²]

Why T20 and T30 instead of a full 60 dB

You almost never have 60 dB between the steady level and the background noise. So the script fits a straight line to part of the decay and extrapolates: T20 fits from −5 to −25 dB and multiplies by 3, T30 fits from −5 to −35 dB and multiplies by 2. The first 5 dB are skipped because the direct sound dominates there. One noise decay is ragged, because noise is random, so the script averages the squared pressure over 5 decays at each of 3 positions before fitting.

The four room conditions · lecture 7 in short

Absorbers eat sound, diffusers deliver it

A porous panel only works where the air is moving, and at a hard wall the air stands still. Diffusers absorb almost nothing but steer trapped sound into the absorbers.

The six wall panels are porous absorbers (mineral wool or foam): air pushed through the fibres loses energy as heat by friction. In front of a hard wall the particle velocity is zero at the wall and largest a quarter-wavelength away. A thin panel on the wall therefore sits in still air at low frequency and does almost nothing there.

Why one absorbing wall is a problem. All six panels go on the wall opposite the door. Sound bouncing between the two side walls (or floor and ceiling) never touches them, so it decays at the slow empty-room rate. The decay becomes two lines, a fast start and a slow tail, and T30 comes out longer than T20. Diffusers on the hard walls scatter that trapped sound towards the absorbers. Part C · about 20 minutes · vacuum cleaner

The room as a sound-power meter

In steady state, power in equals power absorbed. Measure the average pressure, know the absorption from T, and the source's sound power follows.

Sound power W is what the source emits in total, a property of the source alone; sound pressure depends on the room and where you stand. In the diffuse field ⟨p²⟩ = 4ρc·W/A. Put in A = 0.161V/T and take decibels:

LW = Lp − 14 dB + 10·log10(V/1 m³) − 10·log10(T/1 s)

The −14 is 10·log10(0.161/4) = −13.95 dB; the ρc term vanishes because (20 µPa)²/1 pW = 400 ≈ ρc. Then A-weight each octave and add the bands as energies. Average p² over the three positions before taking the log. The calculator below starts with made-up example numbers; type in yours after the lab.

What limits this measurement (good for the discussion)

Only three microphone positions and one source position (the guide calls the method simplified). At 125 Hz the room is below the Schroeder frequency, so the field isn't diffuse and that band is the least reliable. The microphones must be in the reverberant field: the reverberation distance √(A/16π) is only about 0.1–0.2 m in this hard room, so anywhere a metre from the cleaner is fine.

    The questions the guide asks

    Why are corners good positions?

    Every mode has a pressure maximum at every rigid wall, so a corner is a maximum for all modes at once: the speaker there excites them all and an ear there hears them all.

    What are good positions for the diffusers?

    On the hard parallel surfaces the absorbers don't cover (the side walls, floor and ceiling), where the flutter paths bounce. Their job is to scatter that trapped energy towards the absorbing wall. Keep them away from the microphone positions.

    What does "healthy" mean for a decay?

    A flat level before the cut, a straight drop in dB over at least 20–30 dB, a flat noise floor at least 10 dB below the fit range, no clipping and no bumps from talking or doors. Some beating at 125 Hz is normal: only a few modes fall in that band.

    Why do T20 and T30 differ?

    If the decay bends (fast start, slow tail from paths that miss the absorbers), T30 includes more of the slow part and comes out longer. If the SNR is below about 45 dB, the −35 dB point sinks into the noise floor and T30 reads long for a different reason. Use T20 where T30 isn't trustworthy, and say which you used.

    How does one decay compare with the 15-decay average?

    The single decay wobbles several dB around the line because each burst of noise is random; averaging p² over decays and positions smooths it and also averages over the room. Fig. B2 shows both.
