Part 2 - How the impedance is measured

The amplifier drives the speaker through a known resistor R. The card records the amplifier voltage V_amp (AI0) and the speaker voltage V_loud (AI1). The same current flows through R and the speaker, so
Two things went wrong on the day and are corrected in the files:
  • The script was given R = R_E instead of the 32.9 ohm resistor.
  • AI0 sat on a 9.4 V DC offset and read only HALF the amplifier voltage (a bridged amplifier output). So V_amp = 2*AI0.
Check that the stored Z is exactly this formula, on the tweeter:
R = 32.9 % the series resistor (ohm)
R = 32.9000
G = tw.specn(:,2) ./ (2*tw.specn(:,1)); % V_loud / V_amp
Z_check = R * G ./ (1 - G);
largest_difference = max(abs(Z_check - tw.Z))
largest_difference = 0
Zero: the file's Z is the formula above. With the factor 2 the curve lands on R_E at low frequency and the woofer's free-air peak lands on the data sheet's 117 ohm. Without it, Z would come out about half as big.

How to read an impedance curve, left to right:
  1. Low frequency: Z = R_E and the phase is 0. Only the wire.
  2. The peak is the mechanical resonance. Through the motor, the mechanical impedance appears inverted on the electrical side:
At resonance Z_M is smallest (just R_MS), so (Bl)^2/Z_M is largest: the cone moves the most and its back-EMF opposes the current the most.
  1. The phase tells you which side of the resonance you are on. Below the peak the cone is stiffness-controlled; 1/(jwC) inverted becomes jw(..), so it looks like an INDUCTANCE (+ phase). Above the peak the cone is mass-controlled and looks like a CAPACITANCE (- phase). The phase crosses 0 going down exactly at the resonance. That is the precise way to read f (the graders want resonances read from impedance and phase, not from a pressure peak).
  2. High frequency: the voice-coil inductance L_E makes Z rise again and the phase go positive.
Read the three peaks:

Tweeter 1.45 kHz, only 6.7 ohm: a small, heavily damped peak (low Q). Midrange 90 Hz, 19 ohm: that is the 5 1/4" driver IN its DALI box, not in free air. The woofer (closed box) peaks at 42 Hz and 123 ohm.
What the project uses: the midrange and tweeter curves, as they are. A passive crossover sees this impedance, not a resistor: around 1.45 kHz the tweeter is 6.7 ohm instead of 3.6, and at 10 kHz the midrange is 3x its R_E. A filter designed for a nominal resistor moves its corner.

Part 2b1 - Woofer T-S parameters in free air (brief, Appendix B)

The method uses only the magnitude curve:
f1 and f2 are where Z = Z_r on each side of the peak. Z_r is the geometric mean, so on a log axis it sits halfway between R_E and Z_max.
k = wf.fn > 5 & wf.fn < 200;
f = wf.fn(k); Zm = abs(wf.Z(k));
[Zmax, i0] = max(Zm)
Zmax = 116.7015
i0 = 96
f_s = f(i0)
f_s = 22.7500
Data sheet: f_s = 23 Hz, Z_o = 117 ohm.
r_c = Zmax / R_E_wf
r_c = 20.4739
Z_r = sqrt(R_E_wf * Zmax)
Z_r = 25.7914
The two crossings of Z_r, by linear interpolation between the two points on either side (lo = last point under Z_r below the peak, hi = first point under Z_r above it):
lo = find(Zm(1:i0) < Z_r, 1, 'last');
f1 = interp1(Zm(lo:lo+1), f(lo:lo+1), Z_r)
f1 = 17.9519
hi = i0 - 1 + find(Zm(i0:end) < Z_r, 1);
f2 = interp1(Zm(hi-1:hi), f(hi-1:hi), Z_r)
f2 = 28.2838
Check: f_s should be the geometric mean of f1 and f2.
sqrt(f1*f2)
ans = 22.5333
Q_MS = f_s * sqrt(r_c) / (f2 - f1)
Q_MS = 9.9633
Q_ES = Q_MS / (r_c - 1)
Q_ES = 0.5116
Q_TS = Q_MS / r_c
Q_TS = 0.4866
Data sheet: Q_MS = 9.12, Q_ES = 0.42, Q_TS = 0.40. Our Q_MS is close; Q_ES and Q_TS are about 20 % higher (see the discussion after V_AS).
What the Q's mean. Q_MS is the suspension's own damping (lossy rubber), Q_ES the damping by the back-EMF driving current through R_E. Q_ES is 20x smaller, so the electrical damping dominates and Q_TS is close to Q_ES.

plot([f1 f_s f2], [Z_r Zmax Z_r], 'o', 'MarkerFaceColor', 'auto');

Notice that at f1 and f2 the phase is about +-45 deg: those are the half-power points of the motional part, the same idea as the -3 dB points of any resonance.

Part 2b3 - Box volume and V_AS (Lecture 8, added-compliance method)

Inner dimensions, no filling, so the effective volume is the physical one:
V_B = 0.435 * 0.45 * 0.45 % (m^3)
V_B = 0.0881
V_B_litre = V_B * 1000
V_B_litre = 88.0875
The box's air spring adds to the suspension's. Springs in parallel add their stiffnesses, i.e. 1/C_total = 1/C_MS + 1/C_box. The mass is the same in both measurements, so f^2 is proportional to the stiffness:
(The group report has this without the square. The square matters: it would give 74 L instead of 209 L.)
alpha = (f_c / f_s)^2 - 1
alpha = 2.3678
V_AS = V_B * alpha
V_AS = 0.2086
V_AS_litre = V_AS * 1000
V_AS_litre = 208.5762
Data sheet: 150 L. The same theory says Q_TC/Q_TS should equal f_c/f_s (both are sqrt(1+alpha)). Check it:
f_c / f_s
ans = 1.8352
Q_TC / Q_TS
ans = 1.7308
They disagree by 6 %. Using the Q ratio instead gives:
V_AS_from_Q = V_B * ((Q_TC / Q_TS)^2 - 1) * 1000 % (litre)
V_AS_from_Q = 175.7964
Why our V_AS is larger than the data sheet's. The method assumes the moving mass is the same in both measurements. It is not quite: in free air the cone carries air mass on both sides, in the box the rear air mass is different (and our "free air" was with the back cover off, the driver still in the box, so its rear sees a short wide tube). Add to that the spread between samples of the same driver (+-15 % on V_AS is normal) and a box that may not be perfectly airtight. The sensible statement is a range: 175-215 L from our data, 150 L on the data sheet.
From V_AS and the cone area the remaining parameters follow:
S_D = pi * (0.211/2)^2 % effective cone area (m^2), data sheet D = 211 mm
S_D = 0.0350
w_s = 2*pi*f_s;
C_MS = V_AS / (rho*c^2) / S_D^2 % (m/N), data sheet 0.95e-3
C_MS = 0.0012
M_MS = 1 / (w_s^2 * C_MS) % (kg), data sheet 50.5 g
M_MS = 0.0407
Bl = sqrt(w_s * R_E_wf * M_MS / Q_ES) % (T m), data sheet 10.1
Bl = 8.0494
R_MS = w_s * M_MS / Q_MS % (kg/s), data sheet 0.80
R_MS = 0.5837
Every one of these inherits V_AS's error: M_MS goes as 1/V_AS, and Bl as sqrt(M_MS/Q_ES). That is why the project should take f_s and the Q's from our measurement and say clearly where V_AS comes from.

Why two peaks. The box air (a spring, C_AB) and the air plug in the vent (a mass, M_AP) form a Helmholtz resonator. Coupled to the cone's own mass-spring, two resonators give two modes: f_L where cone and vent air move together, f_H where they move against each other.
Why the dip is f_b. At the Helmholtz resonance the vent air swings by itself, and the pressure it builds in the box holds the cone still. A still cone makes no back-EMF, so Z drops towards R_E. The phase crosses 0 going UP there (between two peaks it must). The dip in Z is where the vent does all the work, which is why it is the vent tuning.
Read the dip on each curve (search between the two peaks, 15-35 Hz):
k = v160.fn > 15 & v160.fn < 35; [~, i] = min(abs(v160.Z(k))); f160 = v160.fn(k);
fb_160 = f160(i)
fb_160 = 23.3750
k = v200.fn > 15 & v200.fn < 35; [~, i] = min(abs(v200.Z(k))); f200 = v200.fn(k);
fb_200 = f200(i)
fb_200 = 22.7500
k = v240.fn > 15 & v240.fn < 35; [~, i] = min(abs(v240.Z(k))); f240 = v240.fn(k);
fb_240 = f240(i)
fb_240 = 21.5000
The calculation. A Helmholtz resonator:
The air moving in a tube drags extra air along at each end: 0.85a at the flanged end (inside, on the baffle) and 0.61a at the free end. Leaving out this end correction (as the group report does) makes every f_b too high (26.5 / 23.7 / 21.6 Hz).
a_P = 0.065/2 % vent radius (m)
a_P = 0.0325
S_P = pi*a_P^2 % vent area (m^2)
S_P = 0.0033
L = [0.160 0.200 0.240];
fb_calc = c/(2*pi) * sqrt(S_P ./ (V_B * (L + 1.46*a_P)))
fb_calc = 1×3
23.2762 21.3120 19.7737
Measured 23.4 / 22.8 / 21.5 Hz, calculated 23.3 / 21.3 / 19.8 Hz. Both go down as the tube gets longer (more air mass), and the longest tube is the furthest off: the measured dip moves less than the formula says.

Part 3 - Near field at the cone and at the vent

Close to a small source (r << a, ka << 1) the pressure only depends on the volume velocity and the radiation MASS in front of it:
Far away, p_far = j w rho U / (2 pi r): the same j w U, so the near-field curve has the far-field SHAPE. A small room cannot give you an anechoic 20 Hz; a microphone 1 cm from the cone can.
The near-field files hold H = p / V_loud in Pa/V. Both channels are on the same NI card with a shared clock, so the phase is valid here (unlike the sound card in Lab E).

What you see.
  • The cone has a deep, broad notch near f_b: the vent holds it still.
  • The vent is strong from about f_b down: below f_b it is the vent that radiates. Above f_b the vent's own output falls (12 dB/octave), and what the vent microphone hears up there is mostly the cone, 10 cm away.
  • Cone and vent are about 180 deg apart below f_b: the vent air moves out while the cone moves in. That is why a vented box falls at 24 dB/octave below f_b: the two cancel.
  • The sharp wiggle at 56 Hz in every curve is the room, not the speaker (next section).

Part 3 - Adding cone and vent: the far-field response of the vented box

Because p_near ~ U/a, the two near-field pressures cannot be added as they are: the vent is smaller, so the same U gives a higher pressure in front of it. Scale each by its radius to get something proportional to U:
a_D = 0.211/2 % cone radius (m)
a_D = 0.1055
H_sum = (a_D*nc200.H + a_P*nv200.H) / a_D; % normalised to the cone's level
figure('Name', 'labD_fig6_near_field_sum');
semilogx(nc200.fn, 20*log10(abs(nc200.H)), nv200.fn, 20*log10(abs(nv200.H*a_P/a_D)), nc200.fn, 20*log10(abs(H_sum)), 'k', 'LineWidth', 1.4);
grid on; xlim([8 300]); ylim([-35 20]);
xlabel('Frequency (Hz)'); ylabel('|p/V| (dB re 1 Pa/V, cone scale)');
legend('cone', 'vent x a_P/a_D', 'sum = far-field shape', 'Location', 'southeast');
title('Vent 200 mm: cone + vent, each scaled to volume velocity');
The sum is the vented box's far-field response: flat down to about f_b, then a steep 24 dB/octave fall, because below f_b the vent and cone are out of phase and cancel. In the project this curve is what the woofer's circuit analogy has to reproduce.

Part 3 - Room modes

The lab is a rectangular room 3.35 x 3.05 x 3.09 m. Its axial modes are half a wavelength across each dimension:
room = [3.35 3.05 3.09];
f_axial = c ./ (2*room)
f_axial = 1×3
51.2239 56.2623 55.5340
3.05 m and 3.09 m are nearly equal, so two modes stack at 55.5-56.3 Hz. A strong double mode is exactly what the near-field curves show at 56 Hz. The impedance curves do not show it: it is acoustic, not electrical.
