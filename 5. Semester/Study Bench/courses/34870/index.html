Every microphone and loudspeaker in this course is one circuit, seen from three sides.

Springs, masses and air cavities obey the same equations as capacitors, inductors and resistors. So the course translates everything into a circuit, solves the circuit, and translates back. The rules of the game

10 ECTS · oral exam 9–10 Dec · lab quizzes count 30 % · groups of 3
The one ideaEverything downstream — microphones, loudspeakers, enclosures, metrology — is built on the analogy framework of lectures 1–3. Learn to draw the circuit and the rest of the course is circuit analysis.

What you are being asked to be able to do

  • Draw the equivalent circuit of a simple mechanical or acoustic system, in either analogy.
  • Predict the frequency response of a dynamic, condenser or MEMS microphone, and say which component moves what.
  • Predict the response and impedance of a moving-coil loudspeaker, alone and in a sealed or vented box.
  • Simulate those circuits in LTspice (Lab A) and measure real ones (Labs B–E).
  • Explain it all out loud — the exam is a 30-minute conversation, not a calculation.

Why the course is hard — and why it isn't

It feels hard because three vocabularies are thrown at you at once, and because there are two ways to draw every circuit. It isn't, because there are only three kinds of element (real, differentiating, integrating), two kinds of source, and two coupling constants (Bl and S). Everything else is bookkeeping.

Analogies: why a spring is a capacitor

Beranek 3.2–3.4 · Leach ch. 3/4 · slides 34870_Lecture1
The one ideaTwo physical systems are analogous when they obey the same equations. A mass obeys f = M\,\frac{du}{dt}; an inductor obeys v = L\,\frac{di}{dt}. Same shape — so we can borrow every circuit tool ever invented.

The pictureThree kinds of element, and only three

Pick the variables first. In each domain there is an across variable (measured between two points) and a through variable (measured through a point), and their product is power. Then every passive element is one of three things, depending on how across relates to through:

elementrelationelectricalmechanicalacousticalwhat it does
real\text{across} = K\cdot\text{through}resistor Rdamper RMporous material RAburns energy
differentiating\text{across} = j\omega K\cdot\text{through}inductor Lmass MMopen tube MAstores kinetic energy
integrating\text{across} = \frac{1}{j\omega K}\cdot\text{through}capacitor Cspring CM = 1/kclosed box CAstores potential energy

This is the impedance analogy: voltage ↔ force ↔ pressure, current ↔ velocity ↔ volume velocity. There is a second convention (below) that flips the mechanical column.

The trick that makes it algebraThe jω method

For a sine wave x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) write the phasor \underline{x} = A e^{j\varphi}. Then differentiating in time is just multiplying by j\omega, and integrating is dividing by it. A differential equation turns into a line of complex algebra, and “impedance” is simply across divided by through at one frequency.

derivative
\frac{d}{dt} \;\to\; j\omega \qquad (+90^\circ,\; +6\ \text{dB/oct})
integral
\int dt \;\to\; \frac{1}{j\omega} \qquad (-90^\circ,\; -6\ \text{dB/oct})
impedance, three worlds
Z = \frac{v}{i}\ [\Omega] \qquad Z_M = \frac{f}{u}\ [\text{Ns/m}] \qquad Z_A = \frac{p}{U}\ [\text{Pa·s/m}^3]
decibels
20\log_{10}\frac{A}{A_{ref}}\ \text{(amplitudes)} \qquad 10\log_{10}\frac{P}{P_{ref}}\ \text{(powers)}

Always name the reference: “dB re 1 V/Pa”, “dB re 20 µPa”.

The two conventionsImpedance analogy vs. mobility analogy

You can map the variables two ways. Map force to voltage and you get the impedance analogy: a mass is an inductor, elements that share a velocity go in series. Map velocity to voltage instead and you get the mobility (through/across) analogy: a mass is now a capacitor to ground, and elements that share a velocity go in parallel. Both are correct. They are duals of each other: every mesh in one is a node in the other.

force fvelocity umass MMcompliance CMdamper RMelements sharing a velocity
impedance analogyvoltagecurrentinductorcapacitorresistor RMin series (one loop current)
mobility analogy (course default for mechanics)currentvoltagecapacitor to groundinductorresistor 1/RMin parallel (one node voltage)

The acoustic side is almost always drawn in the impedance analogy (pressure = voltage), because a closed volume naturally becomes a capacitor to ground. The lectures therefore mix: mobility for mechanics, impedance for acoustics, joined by controlled sources (lecture 4).

The trap

Mass and compliance swap electrical elements between the two analogies. Before you read any value off a circuit, find out which analogy it is drawn in. If a mass is a capacitor hanging to ground, it is mobility. If the masses are inductors in a loop, it is impedance. In LTspice the units change too — a mobility circuit's capacitor value is a mass in kg.

When the analogy breaks

A lumped element assumes the whole thing moves as one: it must be much smaller than the wavelength (rule of thumb: below λ/10), homogeneous, and idealised (rigid, lossless or massless as appropriate). Past that you need distributed models (transmission lines, lecture 3 §4) or FEM/BEM. Lecture 3's tube bench shows exactly where it fails.

Lecture 2 · Thu 3 Sep · FL

Mechanical systems: mass, spring, damper

Beranek 3.2–3.4 · Leach ch. 3/4 · slides 34870_Lecture2 · problems 2.1–2.4
The one ideaA mechanical system has two quantities worth tracking — force and velocity — and three building blocks. Sum the forces on each mass (Newton) and you have written a circuit equation.

The elementsWhat each one is, physically

Mass MM kg

f = j\omega M_M\,u

Hates changing speed. Its velocity is always measured against the fixed world, so one terminal is always ground. Rigid body: M_M = \rho V.

Compliance CM m/N

f = \dfrac{u}{j\omega C_M}

A spring's softness, C_M = 1/k. Stores energy when squeezed. Elastic rod: C_M = L/(ES). Responds to the relative motion of its two ends.

Damper RM Ns/m

f = R_M\,u

Friction. Turns motion into heat. Viscous cylinder: R_M \approx 2\pi\mu r L/g. In the mobility analogy it is drawn as a resistor of value 1/R_M.

The pictureOne resonator, drawn both ways

Mass on a spring, with a damper, driven by a force. Newton: f = f_M + f_C + f_R = \left(j\omega M_M + \frac{1}{j\omega C_M} + R_M\right)u = Z_M\,u. All three elements share the same velocity u — so in the impedance analogy they are in series, and in the mobility analogy they hang in parallel off the single node u.

Notice the ground symbol in the mechanical sketch: the zero-velocity reference. Always draw it. The same discipline returns as “always ground the compliance” for acoustic volumes.

SourcesForce sources, velocity sources, and real ones

An ideal force source pushes with a set force whatever the velocity (a Lorentz force on a coil, a Coulomb force). An ideal velocity source imposes a motion whatever it costs (a shaker table, a rotating cam). Real sources are an ideal one plus an output impedance Z_0, and Thévenin ↔ Norton works exactly as in electronics:

impedance analogy
f_{Th} = Z_0\,u_{No}

force source in series with Z0 ⇔ velocity source in parallel with Z0

mobility analogy
u_{Th} = Y_0\,f_{No}

the same statement with the roles of across and through swapped

resonance & Q of a series resonator
f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{M_M C_M}} \qquad Q = \frac{1}{R_M}\sqrt{\frac{M_M}{C_M}}

Q is how many times the stored energy exceeds the energy lost per radian; bandwidth is f0/Q.

Worked in classThe shaker: two masses, three features

A shaker (an electrodynamic actuator, really a loudspeaker) drives a device under test through a stiff rod, the “stinger”. Resonator R = 40 Ns/m, M = 100 g, C = 10⁻⁵ m/N, stinger C = 10⁻⁸ m/N, DUT 250 g. The impedance plot shows three features, and reading such a plot is a skill you will use all term:

The trap

Problem 2.2 asks for k = 1000 N/m — but the circuit wants compliance, C_M = 1/k = 1\ \text{mm/N}. Stiffnesses add when springs act in parallel (a spring behind a spring is stiffer); compliances add when they are in series. The same inversion bites again for acoustic boxes in lecture 4.

Acoustic systems: a tube is a mass, a box is a spring

Beranek 2.4 · Leach 3.3, 3.7, 2.9–2.10 · Jacobsen 1.6, 9.2 · problems 2.1–2.4
The one ideaAir in a short open tube sloshes back and forth as one lump — inertia, so a mass. Air in a closed box compresses and pushes back — a spring. Derive both from the plane-wave tube, and you also learn exactly when they stop being true.

The elementsPressure and volume velocity

Acoustic mass MA kg/m⁴

M_A = \dfrac{\rho\, l^*}{S}

An open tube of effective length l* and area S. Long and narrow → heavy. The effective length includes end corrections (below).

Acoustic compliance CA m⁵/N

C_A = \dfrac{V}{\rho c^2}

A closed volume V of any shape. Bigger box → softer spring. Always drawn to ground — it pushes against still air.

Acoustic resistance RA Pa·s/m³

p = R_A\,U

Felt, mineral wool, cloth, narrow slits: viscous and thermal losses. No clean formula — measured and fitted. Pressure and flow in phase.

Constants used everywhere in this course: ρ = 1.18 kg/m³, c = 344 m/s, so ρc² = 1.40×10⁵ Pa.

The recipeHow every acoustic element is derived (memorise this)

  1. Write pressure and volume velocity for the geometry (start from the plane wave in a tube).
  2. Find the impedance Z(\omega) = p/U at the input.
  3. Split it into a real part, a jω part and a 1/jω part.
  4. Assign RA, MA, CA to those parts — and note the frequency range where the split is valid.
open tube (pressure release at the end)
Z(0) = j\frac{\rho c}{S}\tan(kl^*) \;\approx\; j\omega\frac{\rho l^*}{S} \quad (kl \ll 1)

so a short open tube is a pure mass

closed tube (rigid wall at the end)
Z(0) = -j\frac{\rho c}{S}\cot(kl) \;\approx\; \frac{\rho c^2}{j\omega S l} + \frac{j\omega\rho l}{3S}

a compliance plus a small series mass — the mass correction is usually dropped

infinite tube (no reflection)
Z(0) = \frac{\rho c}{S}

purely resistive: the characteristic impedance; nothing stores energy if the wave never returns

The pictureThe Helmholtz resonator

Neck = mass, cavity = spring, in series, driven by a volume velocity from outside. That is why a bottle gives one tone when you blow across it, why a bass-reflex port has a tuning frequency, and why a dynamic microphone can be given a low-frequency boost with a tube (lecture 4).

Worked in classProblem 2.3 — a box with two tubes

A 23 L box with a wide tube (Ø 10 cm, l* = 12 cm) gives M_{A1} = 18.0 kg/m⁴, C_A = 1.65\times10^{-7} m⁵/N and a Helmholtz frequency of 92.4 Hz. Add a narrow tube (Ø 1 cm, same length): its mass is 100× larger, M_{A2} = 1803, because S \propto a^2.

  • Driven from the wide tube: the pressure in the box peaks at a low anti-resonance around 9.2 Hz (narrow tube + box), and the input impedance dips at the main resonance of 92.8 Hz — barely moved, because the heavy narrow tube hardly loads the light wide one.
  • Driven from the narrow tube: the anti-resonance now comes from the wide tube + box pair, at 92.4 Hz, right on top of the 92.8 Hz resonance. Which opening you drive decides the shape — a preview of driver vs. port in a vented loudspeaker.
The trap

Always ground the compliance. A capacitor that represents an enclosed volume must have one terminal on the reference pressure. Draw it floating between two tubes and the topology is simply wrong. Called out explicitly on the slides; the easiest way to lose Lab A points.

Real openingsEnd corrections and radiation impedance

An open end does not stop at the rim: the air just outside moves too. That extra “added mass” is captured by lengthening the tube to an effective length l*:

end typeadded massadded length
flanged / baffled (opens into a wall)\frac{8}{3\pi^2}\frac{\rho}{a}0.85\,a
unflanged / free (opens into space)0.6133\frac{\rho}{\pi a}0.61\,a
tube flanged on one end, free on the otherl^* = l + 1.46\,a

The same physics, seen from a vibrating piston instead of a tube mouth, is the radiation impedance: the load the air puts on every loudspeaker cone and microphone diaphragm.

PreviewProblem 2.4 — the inside of a dynamic microphone

Diaphragm → small volume V1 → canal with felt → large volume V2. Read straight off: C_{A1} to ground, R_A in series, C_{A2} to ground. A compliance–resistance–compliance low-pass: at high frequency the first cavity shunts the flow before it reaches the second. That is exactly why a dynamic mic's top end rolls off, and lecture 4 builds the whole microphone around it.

Transducers: two constants join three worlds

Beranek 3.5–3.7, ch. 5 · Leach 4.4–4.7, 5.1–5.4 · problems 4.1–4.4 · Lab A parts 3–4
The one ideaA transducer is a pair of controlled sources — one per direction — whose gain is a single physical constant: Bl for a voice coil, S for a diaphragm. Draw that pair right (with the right signs) and a whole microphone is one circuit you can simulate.

The pictureThe chain

A diaphragm of area S turns pressure into force (f = S\,p) and velocity into volume velocity (U = S\,u). A coil of length l in a field B turns current into force (Lorentz, f = Bl\,i) and velocity into voltage (Faraday, v = Bl\,u). Both effects are always present at once, in the same wire — so both must be drawn.

The rule that explains loudspeakersScaling vs. inversion

What does one domain see of the next? It depends on whether the coupling pairs like with like.

diaphragm: a transformer
Z_{M,A} = S^2\,Z_A = S^2\,(Z_{Af} + Z_{Ab})

f = Sp pairs across with across, U = Su through with through. Impedances just scale by S². Front and back loads are in series (same U flows through both).

voice coil: a gyrator
Z_{E,M} = \frac{(Bl)^2}{Z_M} = (Bl)^2\,Y_M

f = Bl\,i pairs across with through. A gyrator maps impedance to admittance: a mechanical series resonance becomes an electrical parallel resonance — an impedance peak.

what each element becomes at the terminals
C_{mot} = \frac{M_M}{(Bl)^2},\quad L_{mot} = (Bl)^2 C_M,\quad R_{mot} = \frac{(Bl)^2}{R_M}

the “motional” parallel RLC. From one impedance sweep you can read fs, Q and, with a known added mass, all the Thiele–Small parameters — no microphone needed.

Why you should care about the inversion
  • Damping flows backwards through the gyrator. A low impedance across the terminals short-circuits the motional RLC and brakes the cone: (Bl)^2/(R_e + R_{load}). That is why a voltage amplifier controls a woofer, why shorting a speaker's terminals makes the cone stiff to push, and why R_L appears inside the microphone's R_{MT} below.
  • Blocked vs. motional. Clamp the diaphragm (Z_M \to \infty) and only R_e + j\omega L_e is left. Free minus blocked is pure motion — that is how Bl is measured.

Lecture 4B · VCHThe dynamic pressure microphone

A moving coil on a diaphragm, a magnet, a closed volume behind the diaphragm, and a piece of felt for damping. Three domains, wired with the sources above:

Write one loop equation per domain, eliminate p_D, u_D and i, and the whole thing collapses into one series resonator with three totals:

transfer function
\frac{e}{p_i} = \frac{R_L}{R_E+R_L}\cdot\frac{-Bl\,S_D}{j\omega M_{MT} + R_{MT} + \dfrac{1}{j\omega C_{MT}}}

transduction factor over the total mechanical impedance — a second-order band-pass

total mass
M_{MT} = M_{MD} + S_D^2 M_{A1}

diaphragm + coil, plus the air it drags in front (radiation mass)

total resistance
R_{MT} = R_{MS} + S_D^2 R_{AF} + \frac{(Bl)^2}{R_E + R_L}

suspension + felt (through S²) + electrical braking (through (Bl)²)

total compliance
\frac{1}{C_{MT}} = \frac{1}{C_{MS}} + \frac{S_D^2}{C_{AB}}

suspension spring and air spring act in parallel, so their stiffnesses add

sensitivity and bandwidth
M \approx \frac{Bl\,S_D}{R_{MT}} \qquad f_b - f_a = \frac{f_0}{Q},\quad f_a f_b = f_0^2

high Q: sensitive but narrow; low Q: wide but deaf. One knob — the felt — trades them.

Problems 4Two tricks for more bandwidth

  • Split the back volume (2c). A small V1 right behind the diaphragm, a tube with damping, then a large V2. At low frequency the tube conducts and both volumes count. At high frequency the tube's mass blocks V2, the diaphragm sees only the stiff V1, and a new higher resonance props up the top end. Put the damping in the tube, or you get a notch instead of a lift.
  • Vent the large cavity (2d). A tube from outside into V2 makes a Helmholtz resonator that boosts the response around its frequency (≈ 100–300 Hz), at the price of a steeper roll-off below it — the same physics as a bass-reflex box.

Worked result from the vault: with both tricks the Problems 4 mic goes from a 292 Hz – 5 kHz hump to flat ±2 dB from 100 Hz to 10 kHz, trading sensitivity (1.6 → 0.67 mV/Pa) for flatness. That is how real broadband dynamic mics are built.

The trap

Signs. The reaction force f = S\,p must oppose the motion, and the back-EMF must oppose the current. Get one backwards and the acoustic load becomes a negative damper: the simulation blows up instead of settling. Sanity-check every coupled circuit by drawing it in both analogies (Problem 4.4) — they must give the same curve.

Directional microphones and the condenser microphone

Beranek 5.2–5.6 · Leach 4.6, 5.5, 5.9–5.11 · problems 5.1–5.2 · Lab C
The one ideaOpen the back of the diaphragm and the microphone hears the difference in pressure — it becomes directional. Swap the voice coil for a charged capacitor and the transduction law gains a 1/jω — the response becomes a low-pass instead of a band-pass.

Part 1Gradient microphones

If sound also reaches the back after an extra path \Delta l, the diaphragm feels p_D = p_F - p_B. For a plane wave (p \propto e^{-jkx}) that is

p_D = jk\,\Delta l\,p\cos\theta

Two consequences fall out at once. The \cos\theta is a figure-8 pattern. The k = \omega/c means the drive rises with frequency — so a gradient mic with the same moving system as lecture 4B is a high-pass: below resonance the response drops at 12 dB per octave. A ribbon microphone is the purest example: the corrugated aluminium ribbon is diaphragm, coil and suspension in one.

Part 1, continuedCombination microphones: the cardioid family

Add an internal delayed path \Delta l' (a small damped duct — Problem 2.4's low-pass again) alongside the external one. The diaphragm now sees a pressure term plus a gradient term:

p_D = jk\,p\,\Delta l'\,(1 + B\cos\theta), \qquad B = \frac{\Delta l}{\Delta l'}, \qquad R(\theta) = \frac{1 + B\cos\theta}{1 + B}
Two meanings of “pressure microphone”

Audio people say pressure mic for any closed-back microphone. Measurement people say pressure mic for one designed to be flat to the pressure actually at the diaphragm (flush-mount, couplers, diffuse field) — as opposed to a free-field mic, which is deliberately shaped to cancel the pressure build-up in front of its own body at high frequency (the T(s) correction). Same words, different design targets. Don't mix them in the exam.

Part 2The condenser microphone, from a capacitor to a circuit

Statically it is a parallel-plate capacitor: diaphragm at rest distance x_0 from the back plate, charged to E volts, C_{E0} = \varepsilon_0 S/x_0. Let the diaphragm move by x(t) and both capacitance and charge become time-varying: Q + q = C_E(t)\,(E + e). Nonlinear — so linearise for small signals (drop products of small quantities):

electrical side (from Q = CV)
e = \frac{1}{j\omega C_{E0}}\,i + \frac{E}{j\omega x_0}\,u

a capacitor, plus a voltage set by the velocity — but with a 1/jω: it really follows displacement

mechanical side (from energy, f = −dW/dx)
f = -\frac{E}{j\omega x_0}\,i - \frac{1}{j\omega C_M}\,u

the diaphragm's own spring, plus an electrostatic force set by the charge (the integral of i)

the coupling constant
\frac{E}{x_0}\ \ [\text{V/m}]

plays the role Bl played: 200 V across 20 µm is 107 V/m

SPICE wants frequency-independent sources, so move each 1/jω into the neighbouring element. Both sides become a capacitor in parallel with a controlled current source — a Norton pair:

Because both couplings pair like with like (no gyrator this time), the mechanics reflect into the electrical side without inversion. Add the acoustics — front air mass, the thin gap in front of the back plate (CAB1, usually negligible), the back-plate holes (RAS, MAS), the back volume (CAB2) — and with a large load resistor (i \approx 0) the whole thing collapses again to one resonator:

open-circuit transfer function
e_{oc} = -\frac{E\,S_D}{j\omega x_0\left[j\omega M_{MT} + R_{MT} + \dfrac{1}{j\omega C_{MT}}\right]}\,T(j\omega)\,p_i

the extra 1/jω on top turns the band-pass into a low-pass

totals
M_{MT} = M_{MD} + S_D^2(M_{A1} + M_{AS}),\quad R_{MT} = R_{MD} + S_D^2 R_{AS},\quad \frac{1}{C_{MT}} = \frac{1}{C_{MD}} + \frac{S_D^2}{C_{AB2}}
sensitivity (flat band)
M = \frac{E\,S_D\,C_{MT}}{x_0}

volts × area × softness ÷ gap. Flat below f0: compliance-controlled.

Three things the lecturer added that are not in the books
  • Losses live in the gap, not the holes. The textbook puts RAS on the back-plate holes; in a real capsule most damping is squeeze-film loss in the thin front gap. You still calculate with the textbook topology, but know it is a simplification.
  • Lab C recipe for fs and Q. Find the frequency where the phase has dropped 90° from mid-band: that is fs, bump or no bump. Then Q is the linear ratio of sensitivity at fs to sensitivity in the flat band — for an overdamped capsule that is less than 1, i.e. negative dB.
  • The load resistor sets the bass. The capsule is a few pF driving R_L'; that is a high-pass with corner 1/(2\pi R_L' C_{E0}). The “large polarization resistor” is not just about not loading the bias supply — it keeps the output flat down to 20 Hz.
Lecture 6 · Thu 17 Sep · VCH · three decks: 6A scattering, 6B metrology and calibration, 6C intro to Labs B and C

The microphone disturbs what it measures, and how we know what it measures

Leach 2.15, 5.2 · Jacobsen eq. 7.6 · BIPM SI Brochure, GUM, VIM · B&K Microphone Handbook · Problems 6 · Labs B and C
The one ideaAt high frequency a microphone is an obstacle: the pressure on its diaphragm is up to 10 dB above the field it was put into. That is the T(j\omega) left unexplained in lecture 5, the difference between a free-field and a pressure microphone, and what Lab B measures. The rest of the lecture is how a measured number earns trust: units, uncertainty, traceability, calibrators.

Part A · the pictureTwo microphones for two fields

Free-field microphone

Reports the pressure that was there before the microphone arrived. Its own response droops at high frequency to cancel the pressure build-up its body causes. Point it at the source.

Pressure microphone

Used where it cannot disturb anything: flush in a wall, closing a small cavity (couplers, calibrators, ear simulators). No compensation — flat to the pressure on the diaphragm.

Size decides

What counts is the diameter in wavelengths, D/\lambda or kR. Bigger capsule: more sensitivity, more field disturbance, lower maximum frequency. 1″, ½″, ¼″, ⅛″ trade those off.

The scattered wave is the reflection from the front face plus waves diffracted from the edges. The slides' numerical result for the pressure at the centre of the diaphragm: about 0 dB below kR = 0.3, +4 dB at kR = 1, a first peak of +10 dB near kR = 3, a dip to −1 dB near 6, and +10 dB again near 9.

The approximationDiaphragm reflection only

Ignore the edges and treat the capsule front as a plane reflector. Incident volume velocity U on the diaphragm area, reflected U_r, diaphragm U_t = U - U_r:

incident and reflected
p_i = \frac{\rho c}{S}U, \qquad U_r = \frac{Z_{ad} - \rho c/S}{Z_{ad} + \rho c/S}\,U

the ordinary reflection coefficient, with the diaphragm's acoustic impedance as the wall

pressure on the diaphragm
p_D = p_i + Z_{ar}U_r

the reflected flow drives the radiation impedance in front of the capsule

stiff diaphragm: Z_ad → ∞, R = 1
p_D = p_i\left(1 + \frac{Z_{ar}}{\rho c/S}\right) = T(s)\,p_i

Leach eq. 5.1. T \to 1 at low frequency, T \to 2 (+6 dB) when Z_{ar} \to R_{A2} = \rho c/S

In the circuit: put a copy of the radiation network M_{A1} \parallel [R_{A2} + (R_{A1} \parallel C_{A1})] in series with the source p_i and drive it with a G source of gain 1/R_{A2} controlled by p_i (the generator called Gpb in the course circuit). The current p_i/R_{A2} through Z_{ar} adds p_i Z_{ar}/R_{A2} — exactly T.

The trap

Every one of these slides carries the same red line: plane reflection — the effect of the corners is not included. The model tops out at +6 dB; the real capsule reaches +10 dB and then oscillates. Use T(s) up to the first peak, and say so. Also: the free-field correction is defined as the ratio between the undisturbed field and the pressure at the diaphragm, not the microphone's output.

Part B · metrologyUnits, uncertainty, traceability

SI. Metre and kilogram from 1795, the Metre Convention and the BIPM in 1875, the SI in 1960, and since 20 May 2019 every unit is defined by seven natural constants (ΔνCs, c, h, e, k, NA, Kcd). TraceabilityFrom the national standard to your sound level meter

National standard (primary calibration) → secondary standard → working standard → measurement instrument. National institutes (DFM in the cellar of building 352, NPL, PTB, NIST…) sit in regional bodies under the BIPM and check each other in key comparisons.

The acoustic primary standard is reciprocity. Three condenser microphones are measured in pairs, one emitting, one receiving. Lab B: a microphone is an obstacle, measured on a wooden one 20 times too big

brief: Labs/34870_Lab_B_CylinderScattering_E2026.pdf · vault: Lab B - Preparation.md, Lab B - Runthrough.md · data, scripts, BEM and reference report: 5. Semester/Electroacoustics/Labs/Lab B/ · theory: lecture 6A
The one ideaOnce a microphone is not small compared with the wavelength, sound reflects off it and the pressure on its own diaphragm is higher than the pressure that would be there without it. That increase, versus frequency and angle, is the free-field correction. Everything depends on ka = 2\pi f a / c, so a 250 mm wooden cylinder at 1.3 kHz behaves exactly like a 1/2 inch microphone at 26 kHz, and an ordinary small microphone can play the role of one point on its "diaphragm".

Theory 1 · the field we wantWhat "free field" means, and how Part 0 proves we have one

A free field is sound travelling away from its source with nothing to reflect it: no walls, no floor, no stand. The anechoic room fakes that with deep absorbing wedges. A wedge absorbs well only when it is deep compared with the wavelength (roughly λ/4), so below a cut-off frequency the walls start reflecting again. For room 028 that is 125 Hz (λ = 2.7 m): below it, ripples in any measured ratio are the room, not the physics.

In a free field a small source radiates a spherical wave. The same power spreads over a sphere of area 4πr², so the intensity falls as 1/r² and the pressure as 1/r. Between two distances the level changes by

spherical spreading
|p(r)| \propto \frac{1}{r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta L = 20\log_{10}\frac{r_1}{r_2}

−6 dB per doubling of distance

Part 0, reference 2.8 m (mean 125–200 Hz)
1.4\ \text{m}: +6.25\ \text{dB}\ (\tfrac{1}{r}: +6.02) \qquad 0.93\ \text{m}: +9.84\ \text{dB}\ (\tfrac{1}{r}: +9.57)

both within 0.3 dB of 1/r just above the cut-off

So Part 0 checks two things at once: the room behaves as a free field above its cut-off, and the loudspeaker is far enough away to look like a point source. That matters because the BEM model assumes a plane wave, and a spherical wave from 2.8 m away is nearly plane across a 25 cm face. It also settled a file-name typo: the lab PC called the series position "1.8 m", but 1/r from 0.93 m to 1.8 m would be 5.7 dB, not the 9.8 dB we saw, so the series was at 2.8 m (as the tape said).

Theory 2 · what an obstacle doesIncident plus scattered, and why the answer depends on ka

Put a rigid body into a sound field. Air cannot flow into a rigid surface, so the particle velocity normal to it must be zero. To satisfy that, the body sends out a scattered wave, and the pressure anywhere is p = p_i + p_s. How big the scattered wave is depends only on the size compared with the wavelength, the Helmholtz number ka (a = radius):

ka ≪ 1 below ~200 Hz here

The wave is so long it flows round the body as if it weren't there. ps is tiny: 0 dB at every angle. Our measurement: +0.0 to +0.8 dB for 125–200 Hz.

ka ≈ 1 437 Hz for a = 125 mm

The transition. The pressure on the face starts to rise and the curves for different angles separate.

ka ≫ 1 above ~1.5 kHz

The face acts like a wall: incident and reflected wave add, pressure doubling, +6 dB. On top of that the wave diffracted at the rim arrives at the centre in phase from all round the edge: up to +9.9 dB at ka ≈ 3, then ripples.

Angle. Head-on (0°) the face blocks the wave fully: the biggest build-up. As the body turns, the face sees the wave more and more at grazing incidence, and at 90° it is not blocking it at all: within about 2 dB. From behind (180°) the face is in the shadow, yet the centre still gets a little: the edge waves meet on the axis (the "bright spot").

Helmholtz number
ka = \frac{2\pi f a}{c}, \qquad ka = 1 \text{ at } f = \frac{c}{2\pi a}

250 mm mock-up: 437 Hz. Useful band 125 Hz–2 kHz is ka = 0.3–4.6

free-field correction
C_{ff}(f,\theta) = 20\log_{10}\frac{p_{\text{on the diaphragm}}}{p_{\text{free field, mic removed}}}

defined on the diaphragm, not on the microphone's output

scaling: same ka, same curve
f_{mic} = f_{mock}\,\frac{D_{mock}}{D_{mic}}

f·D is constant. 250/12.7 = 19.7 for a 1/2 inch

Theory 3 · why a real microphone caresFree-field, pressure and diffuse-field microphones

A microphone's output follows the pressure on its diaphragm. In a free field that is C_{ff} above the pressure we want to know (the one that was there before the microphone arrived). For a 1/2 inch capsule the build-up is several dB above about 10 kHz. Three ways to live with it:

Free-field type B&K 4133

Pointed at the source. The diaphragm is deliberately over-damped so its own (pressure) response droops by exactly the build-up: droop × Cff = flat. That droop is what Lab C measures.

Pressure type B&K 4134

Flat to the pressure on the diaphragm. Used where it does not disturb the field: flush in a wall, closing a coupler (calibrators, ear simulators).

Diffuse-field type

For reverberant rooms where sound comes from everywhere. Flat for the correction averaged over all angles, which is much smaller than the 0° one.

The mock-up is the same shape as a microphone, 20 times bigger, so Lab B measures a microphone's free-field correction on a scale where a small microphone (the UMIK) can sit on the "diaphragm" and 10 kHz of lab equipment covers the 200 kHz a real 1/2 inch would need.

Theory 4 · the measurementOne ratio, and nothing may move

Theory 5 · the predictionWhat the BEM model computes

The boundary element method solves the wave equation for a single frequency (the Helmholtz equation) outside a body by working only on its surface. The surface is cut into small elements, the rigid-wall condition (zero normal velocity) is imposed on each, and the Helmholtz integral equation gives the pressure on every element. The outgoing-wave condition at infinity is built in, which is why BEM suits scattering in free space. Once the surface pressure is known, the same integral gives the pressure at any field point off the surface.

The course model CylinderPlaneWave is axisymmetric: it meshes only the outline of the cylinder and expands an oblique plane wave in circumferential terms (8 of them), which runs out of accuracy above about 4 kHz at oblique angles. It returns the pressure at the face centre, averaged over the face (what a diaphragm feels), or at a field point, and that last option is what explained our data.

B1Why do you divide by the measurement without the mock-up?everything else cancels

Each measurement is a product of the amplifier, loudspeaker, propagation path and UMIK responses; the mock-up only adds the scattering factor. The ratio leaves exactly that factor, so we never need to know the loudspeaker or microphone response. It only works if nothing moved between the two measurements.

B2What is ka and why is it the only thing that matters?size in wavelengths

ka = 2\pi a/\lambda, the radius measured in wavelengths. The wave equation has no built-in length, so the scattering pattern of a given shape depends only on the size relative to the wavelength. That is why a 250 mm cylinder at 1.3 kHz is a 1/2 inch microphone at 26 kHz.

B3What happens at low and at high ka?0 dB → +6 dB (+ edge)

Low ka: the wave bends round the body, no build-up, 0 dB. High ka: the face reflects like a wall, the reflected and incident waves add, +6 dB; the rim adds diffracted waves that reach the centre in phase, up to about +10 dB at ka ≈ 3. The correction is largest at 0° and small at 90°.

B4Why did you measure at different distances without the mock-up?free field + point source

To check that the level falls as 1/r (−6 dB per doubling): that shows the room is anechoic in that band and the loudspeaker acts as a point source, so the incident wave on the mock-up is (nearly) plane, as the BEM assumes. With 2.8 m as reference we got +6.25 dB at 1.4 m and +9.84 dB at 0.93 m against +6.02 and +9.57 dB from 1/r.

B5Why is there a deep notch near 4 kHz in your 0° curve?λ/4 in front of the face

The UMIK tip was 2–3 cm in front of the face. In front of a hard face the incident and reflected waves form a standing wave with a pressure null a quarter wavelength out: λ/4 = 2.2 cm at 3.9 kHz. The BEM at a field point 3 cm in front reproduces the notch; the face-centre BEM has none.

B6What is the difference between a free-field and a pressure microphone?compensated vs not

A free-field microphone is designed so that its pressure response times the free-field correction is flat at 0°: it reports the field as it was without the microphone. A pressure microphone is flat to the pressure on its diaphragm and is used where it does not disturb the field (couplers, flush in a wall). In Lab C the free-field 4133 droops by about 8.6 dB at 20 kHz in the actuator test for exactly this reason.

B7What does the BEM calculate, and what are its limits here?surface integral equation

It solves the Helmholtz integral equation on the surface mesh of a rigid body in a plane wave, giving the surface pressure and from it the pressure anywhere. Limits: a rigid, ideal cylinder in a plane wave (not a spherical wave from a real loudspeaker), no stand or UMIK body, and only 8 circumferential terms, so oblique curves are unreliable above about 4 kHz.

B8Why is the measured maximum lower than the BEM's?not on the face

The UMIK was not at the face centre but a few cm in front of it, where the build-up is smaller and the phase of the reflected wave differs; the 3 cm field-point BEM gives +8.7 dB at 1.1 kHz, matching our +8.6 dB at 1155 Hz. Lab C: one number from a calibrator, a whole curve from an actuator

brief: Labs/34870_Lab_C_ActuatorCalibration_E2026.pdf · raw data, MATLAB (part1–part4), LTspice and reference report: 5. Semester/Electroacoustics/Labs/Lab C/ · theory: lectures 5 and 6B
The one ideaA calibrator puts a known pressure on the microphone at one frequency: that gives the sensitivity in mV/Pa, traceable to the SI. An electrostatic actuator pulls on the bare diaphragm with an electric field: no sound, no scattering, no room, so it gives the shape of the pressure response over the whole band, but no absolute level. Shape from the actuator, level from the calibrator. Then fs and Q read off the curve tell you what sits behind the diaphragm.

Theory 1 · the transducerHow a condenser microphone turns pressure into volts

A thin, tensioned metal diaphragm sits x_0 \approx 20\ \mu\text{m} in front of a rigid, perforated backplate. The two form a capacitor, C_{E0} = \varepsilon_0 S_D/x_0 \approx 27\ \text{pF}, charged to the polarisation voltage E = 200 V through a resistor of several GΩ. That resistor is so large that the charge cannot change during one period of any audio frequency: the capsule works at constant charge. With Q fixed, the voltage follows the gap, v = Q/C \propto x_0 - x, so a diaphragm displacement x gives

electrical: constant charge
e = E\,\frac{x}{x_0}

the output is proportional to displacement

mechanical: stiffness controlled
x = C_{MT}\,S_D\,p \quad (f \ll f_s)

force SDp on a spring of compliance CMT

so the sensitivity is
M = \frac{e}{p} = \frac{E\,C_{MT}\,S_D}{x_0} = 11.14\ \text{mV/Pa}

with the brief's numbers: E = 200 V, CMT = 18.4 µm/N, SD = 62.9 mm², x0 = 20.77 µm

Electrical E, x₀, CE0

E/x₀ is the coupling: it sets the level, nothing else. Bigger E or smaller gap = more sensitive, until the electrostatic pull collapses the diaphragm onto the backplate.

Mechanical MMD, CMD

The diaphragm: 1.5 mg of mass, its tension a compliance of 0.02 mm/N. Alone it would resonate at 28.4 kHz.

Acoustical MAS, RAS, CAB

Behind the diaphragm, air is squeezed sideways through the thin gap and the backplate holes (mass MAS and viscous loss RAS) into the back volume (126 mm³, a spring CAB). A tiny vent lets the static pressure equalise, so the capsule does not respond to weather: a high-pass corner at a few Hz.

The back volume is a second spring in series with the diaphragm's tension: 1/C_{MT} = 1/C_{MD} + S_D^2/C_{AB}, stiffening it by 9 % (0.02 → 0.0184 mm/N). Our measured phase leads by about +6° at 20 Hz, which is that low-frequency corner (vent, preamplifier input, Nexus high-pass) sitting near 2 Hz.

Theory 2 · the response shapeStiffness below, mass above, damping in between

Seen from the diaphragm everything is in series: one mass (diaphragm plus the air it drags through the backplate), one resistance, one spring. Output follows displacement, and displacement per force is 1/(j\omega Z_M):

series mechanical impedance
Z_M = j\omega M_{MT} + R_{MT} + \frac{1}{j\omega C_{MT}}

M_{MT} = M_{MD} + S_D^2(M_{A1} + M_{AS}), R_{MT} = S_D^2 R_{AS}

second-order low-pass
\frac{e}{p} = \frac{M}{1 - (f/f_s)^2 + j (f/f_s)/Q}

f_s = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{M_{MT}C_{MT}}}, Q = \frac{1}{R_{MT}}\sqrt{\frac{M_{MT}}{C_{MT}}}

reading it off a measurement
\angle H(f_s) = -90^\circ, \qquad Q = \frac{|H(f_s)|}{|H(f \ll f_s)|}

Q is a linear ratio; a response that is down at fs has Q < 1

Theory 3 · the two excitationsCalibrator for the level, actuator for the shape

Sound calibrator / pistonphone

A closed coupler much smaller than the wavelength, so the pressure is uniform and known: the device's certificate states it for a microphone of this type inserted. 94 dB = 1.00 Pa, 114 dB = 10.0 Pa. 1 kHz is the reference frequency of acoustics (A-weighting is 0 dB there, a 1/2 inch microphone's free-field correction is practically 0 dB). 250 Hz is the pistonphone's frequency: a cam-driven piston changes the volume by a known ΔV, p = \gamma P_0 \Delta V/V, so it needs a barometer correction. Our three GRAS 42AG units do both frequencies.

Electrostatic actuator

A slotted metal grid a fraction of a mm above the diaphragm, fed with a high DC plus the AC signal. The electrostatic force is F \propto U_{DC}\,u_{ac}/d^2, spread evenly over the diaphragm and independent of frequency: an equivalent pressure. No sound field, no scattering, no room, so you get the pressure response shape to far above what any calibrator reaches, but not its level: d and the voltages are not known well enough to compute the force in pascals.

That is why the lab combines them: scale the actuator curve so it passes through the calibrator's value at 1 kHz,

M(f) = M(1\ \text{kHz})\,\frac{H_{pv}(f)}{|H_{pv}(1\ \text{kHz})|}

The calibrator gives a pressure sensitivity (closed coupler), and so does the actuator, so the two are compatible for both capsule types. For the free-field 4133 the free-field response would be this times the free-field correction of Lab B.

The diaphragm, the air in the backplate and the back volume make one series mass, resistance and compliance. Displacement per force is CMT/(1 − ω²MMTCMT + jωRMTCMT): flat below fs (spring), −12 dB/octave above (mass), −90° at fs where only the resistance is left.

C4How do you find f_s and Q from your measurement?−90° phase, level ratio

fs where the corrected phase crosses −90°, Q as the linear ratio of the level there to the low-frequency level. Mic 1: 20.2 kHz, Q 0.84. Mic 2: 22.9 kHz, Q 0.34. For Mic 2 a whole-curve fit gives 21.1 kHz, Q 0.39: its damping is not a single lumped resistance.

C5Which microphone is the free-field one, and why is it made like that?Mic 2, over-damped

Mic 2 (4133): it droops early in the actuator (pressure) response, −8.6 dB at 20 kHz. In a free field its own body raises the pressure on the diaphragm by the free-field correction (Lab B), and the droop is designed to cancel it so the free-field response is flat at 0°.

C6Why do you need both a calibrator and the actuator?level vs shape

The calibrator gives a known pressure, so an absolute sensitivity, but at one frequency. The actuator gives the response at all frequencies, without scattering or room effects, but the force in pascals is unknown (grid distance and voltages are not known precisely). Scale the actuator curve to the calibrator value at 1 kHz.

C7Why divide by H_21,ref, and why complex?removes the chain, incl. delay

It removes the Nexus and card responses, including a 3.2 µs delay between the input channels that is −48° of phase at 42 kHz. fs is read from the phase, so without the complex correction it comes out several kHz too low.

C8Your three calibrators agree to 0.2 %. Is the sensitivity then known to 0.2 %?no: repeatability ≠ accuracy

No. 0.2 % is repeatability. Changing the method (250 Hz vs 1 kHz) shifted the result by 4 %, a systematic effect the spread cannot see. The uncertainty is dominated by the calibrators' own (Type B) uncertainty: about ±0.2 dB each, giving U ≈ 2.8 % at k = 2.

C9Why did you drop the top frequencies?above Nyquist

The card samples at 96 kHz, so nothing above 48 kHz can be measured. The 53.6 kHz tone aliases to 42.4 kHz, and the 47.8 kHz tone is on the anti-aliasing filter edge (−5.8 dB in the reference). The usable band is 20 Hz to 42.7 kHz.

C10What does 64 instead of 4 averages buy you?12 dB less noise

Synchronous averaging leaves the multitone unchanged and reduces random noise by √N in amplitude, so 16 times more averages lowers the noise by 10·log₁₀16 = 12 dB. We measured 11.6 dB, showing the noise is random, not a disturbance locked to the signal.

The five rules you can say out loud

  1. Lumped elements need the part to be smaller than about λ/10.
  2. Ground every mass (mobility) and every enclosed volume (compliance).
  3. Check which analogy a circuit is drawn in before reading a single value.
  4. A transducer is two controlled sources; the reaction must oppose the motion.
  5. A diaphragm scales impedances by S²; a voice coil inverts them by (Bl)².
