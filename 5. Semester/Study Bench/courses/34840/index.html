Sound is a small pressure ripple. Three equations, and everything follows.

Newton's law, conservation of mass and an adiabatic gas give the wave equation. Complex notation turns it into algebra. From there: waves that travel, reflect and spread, and the decibel bookkeeping that every noise limit is written in. Every plot below is live.

Plane waves: what sound is, and the equation it obeys

course text pp. 1–8 · slides 34840_Lecture1 + wave-equation supplement · Problems week 1
The one ideaSound is a tiny departure of pressure from the ambient 101 kPa, tied to a tiny back-and-forth motion of the air. The pattern travels at c; the air does not. Newton + mass conservation + an adiabatic gas give \partial^2 p/\partial x^2 = (1/c^2)\,\partial^2 p/\partial t^2.

The quantitiesThree things to keep apart

Sound pressure p Pa

The fluctuation on top of the static pressure p_0. A loud conversation is 0.02 Pa; the pain threshold about 60 Pa; p_0 is 101 300 Pa. Linear acoustics assumes |p| \ll p_0.

Particle velocity u m/s

How fast the air at one spot moves, back and forth along the wave. Millimetres per second. Not the speed of sound: the wave passes through the air, the air stays put on average.

Speed of sound c m/s

How fast the pattern travels: 343 m/s in air at 20 °C, c = \sqrt{\gamma R_s T} with T in kelvin. Sets the wavelength \lambda = c/f and the wavenumber k = 2\pi/\lambda = \omega/c.

Constants the sheets use: c = 343 m/s, ρ₀ = 1.204 kg/m³, so ρ₀c = 413 Pa·s/m; γ = 1.401, p₀ = 101.3 kPa. Every amplitude on the sheets is a peak value.

The derivationThree physical statements, one wave equation

A thin slice of air of area S and thickness dx. Write the density as \rho_0 + \rho', drop products of small quantities.

1 · Newton (Euler's equation)
\rho_0\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}

a pressure gradient accelerates the slice: mass × acceleration = net force

2 · conservation of mass
\frac{\partial \rho'}{\partial t} = -\rho_0\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}

if more air leaves the slice than enters, its density drops

3 · adiabatic gas
p = c^2\rho', \qquad c^2 = \frac{\gamma p_0}{\rho_0}

compressions are too fast to exchange heat, hence γ; compression raises the pressure, p = -\gamma p_0\,\Delta V/V_0

combine: ∂/∂t of 2, ∂/∂x of 1, insert 3
\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial t^2}

anything of the form f(t - x/c) or f(t + x/c) solves it: a shape moving right or left at speed c

The trickComplex notation

Write a harmonic wave as the real part of a complex exponential and keep the common e^{j\omega t} out of sight:

p(x,t) = \Re\{P(x)\,e^{j\omega t}\}, \qquad P(x) = A\,e^{-jkx}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \to j\omega, \qquad \int dt \to \frac{1}{j\omega}

Then the wave equation becomes the Helmholtz equation d^2P/dx^2 + k^2 P = 0, and Euler's equation reads j\omega\rho_0 U = -dP/dx. For a wave travelling towards +x that gives the two relations you use in every problem:

velocity from pressure
U = \frac{P}{\rho_0 c} \qquad (\rho_0 c = 413\ \text{Pa·s/m})

in phase with the pressure; the ratio is the characteristic impedance of the medium. A wave travelling towards −x has the opposite sign.

displacement from velocity
\Xi = \frac{U}{j\omega} \;\Rightarrow\; |\Xi| = \frac{|P|}{\omega\rho_0 c}

lags the velocity by 90° and shrinks with frequency — a subwoofer visibly moves, a tweeter does not

adding waves of the same frequency
P_{tot} = P_1 + P_2, \qquad |a + a e^{-j\theta}| = 2a\left|\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right|

add the complex amplitudes first, take the magnitude last

The traps
  • Particle velocity is not the speed of sound. 1 Pa gives 2.4 mm/s of air motion; the wave still moves at 343 m/s.
  • Kelvin, not celsius, in c = \sqrt{\gamma R_s T}. Ratios of speeds are square roots of ratios of absolute temperatures.
  • Peak, not rms. The sheets give peak amplitudes and want peak answers; do not insert a √2 until lecture 3 asks for levels.
  • Radians. A 179° lag is 179\pi/180 before it goes into an exponential.

Reflection, standing waves, and the spherical wave

course text pp. 8–15 (§1.2) · slides 34840_Lecture2 · Problems week 2
The one ideaTwo travelling waves in opposite directions make a standing wave; a boundary decides how much comes back and with what sign. And once a wave spreads in three dimensions, pressure and velocity stop being in phase near the source.

The pictureIncident plus reflected

P(x) = P_i e^{-jkx} + P_r e^{jkx}, \qquad U_x(x) = \frac{P_i e^{-jkx} - P_r e^{jkx}}{\rho c}, \qquad R = \frac{P_r}{P_i}

The minus sign in the velocity is the whole story: velocity has a direction, pressure does not. At a rigid wall the velocity must vanish, so P_r = P_i, R = +1: pressure antinode at the wall, first pressure node a quarter wavelength away, then every half wavelength. At a pressure-release end (an ideal open tube end) the pressure must vanish, R = -1: the roles swap.

rigid wall
P = 2P_i\cos kx,\quad U = -j\frac{2P_i}{\rho c}\sin kx,\quad d_n = \frac{(2n+1)\lambda}{4}
pressure release
P = -2jP_i\sin kx,\quad U = \frac{2P_i}{\rho c}\cos kx
partial reflection
s = \frac{|P|_{max}}{|P|_{min}} = \frac{1+|R|}{1-|R|}, \qquad |R| = \frac{s-1}{s+1}

the standing wave ratio is how |R| is measured in a tube

Two mediaReflection and transmission at normal incidence

At the interface the pressure is continuous (no net force on a massless surface) and the normal velocity is continuous (the fluids stay in contact). With Z = \rho c for each medium:

from the two continuity conditions
P_i + P_r = P_t, \qquad \frac{P_i - P_r}{Z_1} = \frac{P_t}{Z_2}
reflection and pressure transmission
R = \frac{Z_2 - Z_1}{Z_2 + Z_1}, \qquad T_p = \frac{P_t}{P_i} = 1 + R = \frac{2Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2}

the sheet's "attenuation factor" is P_i/P_t = 1/T_p

power, which is what actually crosses
\tau = \frac{4Z_1Z_2}{(Z_1+Z_2)^2} = 1 - |R|^2

symmetric in the two media; air–water passes 0.1 % of the power either way

The trap

A transmitted pressure larger than the incident one is not a gain of energy. Air into water: R = 0.9994, yet P_t = 2P_i. The pressure doubles because the incident and reflected waves add at the interface; the power that gets through is \tau = 0.11\,\%. Water into air: the same 0.11 % of power, but the pressure drops 1793 times (65 dB). Keep pressure ratios and power ratios in separate columns.

SpreadingThe spherical wave

With spherical symmetry the wave equation for r\,p is one-dimensional, so the outgoing solution is

P(r) = \frac{A\,e^{-jkr}}{r}, \qquad |P(r)| = |P(r_0)|\,\frac{r_0}{r}, \qquad U_r = \frac{P}{\rho c}\left(1 + \frac{1}{jkr}\right), \qquad Z_s = \frac{P}{U_r} = \frac{\rho c}{1 + 1/(jkr)}

Pressure obeys the inverse-distance law (−6 dB per doubling). The particle velocity has an extra term that only matters close to the source, where kr is small: there the velocity is larger than P/\rho c and lags the pressure, the field is reactive, and the plane-wave relation underestimates the motion.

Link to Electroacoustics

P/U_r here is the specific acoustic impedance in Pa·s/m. The lumped circuits of 34870 use pressure over volume velocity, Pa·s/m³: divide by the area the flow passes through. The 1/(jkr) near-field term is exactly the "mass-like" radiation impedance that loads a loudspeaker cone at low frequency.

Levels and measurement: rms, decibels, bands, weighting, time

course text pp. 15–27 · slides 34840_Lecture3 (43) · Problems week 3 · Matlab sound-level-meter exercise
The one ideaA meter squares the pressure, averages it, and takes a logarithm. Independent sounds add as squares; the same tone from two places adds as phasors. Everything else in this lecture is which filter sits before the squaring and which averaging sits after it.

The definitionsFrom pressure to level

rms pressure
p_{rms} = \sqrt{\overline{p^2(t)}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T p^2\,dt}

a sine of amplitude A has p_{rms} = A/\sqrt2

sound pressure level
L_p = 10\log_{10}\frac{p_{rms}^2}{p_{ref}^2} = 20\log_{10}\frac{p_{rms}}{20\ \mu\text{Pa}}

20 µPa ≈ the hearing threshold at 1 kHz; 1 Pa rms is 94 dB

uncorrelated sources add their mean squares
p^2_{rms,tot} = \sum_i p^2_{rms,i} \;\Rightarrow\; L_{tot} = 10\log\sum_i 10^{L_i/10}

+3 dB for two equal sources, +10 dB for ten; the cross terms average to zero

background correction
L_{src} = 10\log\left(10^{L_{tot}/10} - 10^{L_{bg}/10}\right)

only reliable if the background is at least 3 dB below the total; under 0.5 dB correction beyond 10 dB

same tone, two paths (coherent)
|A e^{j\varphi_A} + B e^{j\varphi_B}| \in [\,|A - B|,\ A + B\,]

anywhere between −∞ and +6 dB; the mean-square rule does not apply

other levels
L_I = 10\log\frac{I}{10^{-12}\,\text{W/m}^2}, \qquad L_W = 10\log\frac{P_a}{10^{-12}\,\text{W}}

linear quantities get 20·log, quadratic ones 10·log

FrequencyOctave bands and 1/n-octave bands

Hearing has roughly constant relative bandwidth, so acoustics analyses in constant-percentage bands: an octave is a factor 2, a 1/3-octave a factor 2^{1/3} (almost exactly a tenth of a decade, which is why the standard centre frequencies are 1.25, 1.6, 2, 2.5, 3.15, 4, 5, 6.3, 8, 10 ×10ⁿ).

octave band
f_l = \frac{f_c}{\sqrt2}, \qquad f_u = \sqrt2\,f_c, \qquad f_c = \sqrt{f_l f_u}
1/n-octave band
f_u/f_l = 2^{1/n}, \qquad f_{l,u} = f_c\,2^{\mp 1/2n}, \qquad BW = f_c\left(2^{1/2n} - 2^{-1/2n}\right)
Parseval: bands partition the mean square
p_{rms}^2 = \sum_i p_{rms,i}^2, \qquad L_Z = 10\log\sum_i 10^{L_i/10}, \qquad L_A = 10\log\sum_i 10^{(L_i + K_i)/10}

total level from band levels; A-weighted total by adding the table's K_i per band first

Weighting and timeWhat the meter does before and after squaring

Before: a frequency weighting. A-weighting follows the ear's low-level sensitivity and is the basis of nearly every noise limit (L_A, "dB(A)"); C-weighting is nearly flat and a large L_C - L_A flags low-frequency noise; Z is unweighted. After: a time weighting. Exponential (Fast τ = 125 ms, Slow τ = 1 s) gives a running level; the equivalent level L_{eq,T} averages the squared pressure over a whole interval (30 s for installations, 8 h for a workplace, 24 h for traffic).

exponential time weighting
L_p(t) = 10\log\left[\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{-\infty}^{t} p^2(u)\,e^{-(t-u)/\tau}\,du \Big/ p_{ref}^2\right]

digitally: y_n = \alpha y_{n-1} + (1-\alpha)p_n^2, \alpha = e^{-1/\tau f_s}

equivalent level
L_{eq} = 10\log\left[\frac{1}{t_2 - t_1}\int_{t_1}^{t_2} p^2(t)\,dt \Big/ p_{ref}^2\right]

the level of the rms pressure over the interval — never the average of the decibels

The instrument

A sound level meter is a ½-inch condenser microphone (thin tensioned foil over a charged back plate — the very capsule modelled in 34870 lecture 5), an amplifier, the weighting filter, a squarer, the time-averaging low-pass, and a log converter. Below 1 kHz the 13 mm capsule is omnidirectional; above a few kHz it disturbs the field, which is why free-field, random-incidence and pressure microphones exist. Noise measurements of random signals have a random error that shrinks with bandwidth × averaging time.

Impedance, intensity and sound power

Lecture 4 slides 1–36 · course text: impedance and sound energy · all Week 4 problems and exam examples
The one ideaImpedance tells us how pressure drives motion; only motion in phase with pressure transports energy on average.

The picture: choose the right port variables

ImpedanceDefinitionUnits
Specific / wavez=P/UPa·s/m
MechanicalZ_m=F/vN·s/m
AcousticZ_a=P/Q=Z_m/S^2N·s/m⁵

For uniform motion, Q=SU and F=PS. Both variables transform, so the area gets squared. A mass contributes j\omega M; a spring contributes K/(j\omega). Their reactive impedances cancel at resonance.

Closed tube and cavity limit
Z_{a,\mathrm{in}}=-j\frac{\rho c}{S}\cot(kl)\approx\frac{\rho c^2}{j\omega V}\quad(kl\ll1)

Rigid end: infinite impedance. Input zeros: odd quarter wavelengths. A small sealed cavity acts as an air spring.

Helmholtz neck and cavity
M_a=\frac{\rho l_{\rm eff}}S,\quad K_a=\frac{\rho c^2}V,\quad f_0=\frac{c}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{S}{Vl_{\rm eff}}}
Reading the slides

Slide 14: graph C is the series input impedance, with a minimum at resonance. Slide 16 says 300 ml but substitutes 330 ml: 330 ml gives 199.7 Hz; 300 ml gives 209.5 Hz. Problem 1 has its own 400 ml bottle and gives 334 Hz. An effective neck length already includes the end correction.

Stored energy and energy flow

\overline w_p=\frac{|P|^2}{4\rho c^2},\qquad\overline w_k=\frac{\rho|U|^2}{4}

Peak phasors; energy density in J/m³. The two are equal for a travelling plane wave.

I=\tfrac12\Re\{PU^*\}=p_{\rm rms}u_{\rm rms}\cos\phi

Quadrature motion stores and returns energy. For a plane wave I=p_{\rm rms}^2/(\rho c).

W=\int_S\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\tfrac12|Q|^2\Re\{Z_a\}

The last equality uses a uniform acoustic port. A plane wave through area S gives IS; an outgoing spherical wave gives 4\pi r^2 I_r.

For a spherical wave U_r=(P/\rho c)(1+1/jkr). The near-field term is reactive, so I_r=|P|^2/(2\rho c) still falls as 1/r² and total power stays constant.

Absorbing termination
s=10^{(L_{\max}-L_{\min})/20},\quad |R|=\frac{s-1}{s+1},\quad\alpha=1-|R|^2
Net power in the tube
W_{\rm abs}=\frac{S(p_{i,\rm rms}^2-p_{r,\rm rms}^2)}{\rho c}=\frac{S p_{\max,\rm rms}p_{\min,\rm rms}}{\rho c}
The traps

Use RMS pressure for SPL and the factor ½ with peak phasors for power. A 4 mm peak-to-peak displacement means 2 mm peak. In a standing wave, local pressure squared does not give net intensity. At a transmitting interface, 1-|R|^2 is transmitted energy, not heat absorption. Slide 33's net intensity equals absorbed intensity at the termination; it equals incident intensity only for full absorption.

Radiation: monopoles, dipoles and pistons

Lecture 5 slides 1–42 · course text §1.6, pp. 37–48 (the piston is from the text only) · Week 5 problems 1–5, exam A, MC 1–2
The one ideaA source radiates well only when it pushes a net volume of air and is not small next to the wavelength; a wall, a twin or a baffle just changes how much of its own pressure pushes back in phase with its velocity.

The picture: one monopole, four situations

Situation, kh\ll1, same Q per sourceFar-field pressurePower
One monopole, free field11
Same monopole against a rigid wall (in-phase image)×2 (+6 dB), half space×2 (+3 dB)
Two in-phase monopoles close together×2 (+6 dB), full space×4 (+6 dB)
Two antiphase monopoles (dipole), 2h apart\propto kh\cos\theta\times\tfrac86(kh)^2\ll1

Changes its volume (closed-box loudspeaker, exhaust, bubble) → monopole. Only pushes air sideways (bare driver, string, cymbal) → dipole. A small pulsating sphere is quiet not because its surface stands still but because its surface pressure is 90° ahead of the velocity: a mass-like load, Z_{a,r}=\frac{\rho c}{4\pi a^2}\frac{jka}{1+jka}, almost no resistance.

Monopole (volume velocity Q, peak)
\hat p=\frac{j\omega\rho Q}{4\pi r}e^{j(\omega t-kr)},\qquad I_r=\frac{p_{\rm rms}^2}{\rho c}\ \text{at any }r
P_a=\frac{\rho c k^2|Q|^2}{8\pi}

Pressure follows the volume acceleration \omega Q: flat power needs excursion falling as 1/f^2.

Levels
L_I=L_p-10\log_{10}\frac{\rho c}{400},\qquad L_W=L_I+10\log_{10}(4\pi r^2)

At 1 m: L_W=L_I+11 dB. References I_0=W_0=10^{-12}.

Near a rigid wall (image 2h away)
P_a=\frac{\rho ck^2|Q|^2}{8\pi}\left(1+\frac{\sin2kh}{2kh}\right)

→ ×2 (+3 dB) for kh\to0; no effect past h\approx\lambda/4.

Dipole (antiphase twin, 2h apart)
\hat p\approx\frac{\rho c\,hk^2Q}{2\pi r}\cos\theta,\qquad P_a\approx\frac{\rho c\,h^2k^4|Q|^2}{6\pi}

Exact: \frac{\rho ck^2|Q|^2}{4\pi}\left(1-\frac{\sin2kh}{2kh}\right). Power \propto f^4.

Baffled piston, far field
\hat p=\frac{j\omega\rho Q}{2\pi r}\left[\frac{2J_1(ka\sin\theta)}{ka\sin\theta}\right]e^{j(\omega t-kr)},\quad Q=\pi a^2U

−3 dB at ka\sin\theta=1.62, first null at 3.83.

Radiation impedance
Z_{a,r}=\frac{\rho c}{\pi a^2}\left[1-\frac{J_1(2ka)}{ka}+j\frac{H_1(2ka)}{ka}\right]\xrightarrow{ka\ll1}\frac{\rho ck^2}{2\pi}+j\omega\frac{8\rho}{3\pi^2a}

Air mass 8\rho a^3/3 per side; Q(f)=(ka)^2/R_1\to2.

The traps
  • Three different Q's. Volume velocity (m³/s), quality factor, and the directivity factor Q(f) all appear in this chapter.
  • The wall changes the emitted power (fixed Q). "Same power squeezed into half the space" would give +3 dB SPL; the real answer at kh\ll1 is +6 dB SPL and +3 dB power.
  • Wall vs neighbour: a wall doubles the power, a real in-phase twin quadruples the total. Both double the far-field pressure.
  • What h means: the wall distance, or half the dipole spacing. Problems 3–5 all have h=0.1 m.
  • Peak vs RMS: \rho ck^2|Q|^2/8\pi uses peak |Q|; with RMS it is /4\pi. Exam A's "magnitude" is the peak, 8.94 Pa, not the 6.33 Pa RMS.
  • Baffled-piston resistance is \rho ck^2/2\pi, twice the free sphere's /4\pi: the baffle is an image.
  • The small-kh dipole formula overestimates: 4 % at kh=0.46, 22 % at kh=1.
The loudspeaker link

A bare driver is a dipole (front and back in antiphase), so its bass falls as f^4; a closed box makes it a monopole. Above resonance a driver is mass-controlled, so a flat drive gives a flat \omega Q and flat power, at the price of excursion rising as 1/f^2: bigger cones for more bass. The radiation mass 8\rho a^3/3 (0.88 g per side for a 6.5″ cone) is the air load inside M_{MS} in 34870. Slide 37's comparison (40 Hz, |Q|=0.1 m³/s, h = 5 cm): monopole 109.5 dB, at a wall 112.5 dB, dipole 82.0 dB L_W. Slide 23 misses a minus sign: e^{-jkr_1}.

Room modes and the diffuse sound field

Lecture 6 slides (53 pages) · course text ch. 3 (J. H. Rindel), pp. 81–101 · Week 6 problems 1–3 and Solution 6
The one ideaAt low frequency a room is a 3-D organ pipe with a few separate modes; at high frequency the modes are so dense that you just balance energy: power in equals power absorbed, and that gives both the reverberation time and the level.

The picture: two regimes, split at the Schroeder frequency

Low frequency: wave theoryHigh frequency: statistical theory
What you seeseparate peaks, 20–30 dB between them; depends on positionsmooth response; same p^2 everywhere
Key resultf_{n_x n_y n_z}=\frac c2\sqrt{(n_x/l_x)^2+(n_y/l_y)^2+(n_z/l_z)^2}T=0.16V/A,\quad p^2=4P_a\rho c/A
Boundarymodal density dN/df\approx4\pi Vf^2/c^3 times bandwidth 2.2/T ≈ 3 → f_S\approx2000\sqrt{T/V}

Axial modes (one non-zero index) bounce between one pair of walls and are the strongest; tangential modes (two) and oblique modes (three) are weaker. Every mode has a pressure maximum in the corners; an odd index puts a nodal plane through the centre. Pressures are RMS throughout this lecture.

Mode count
N(f)\approx\frac{4\pi V}{3}\Big(\frac fc\Big)^3+\frac{\pi S}{4}\Big(\frac fc\Big)^2+\frac L8\frac fc

S = total surface area, L=4(l_x+l_y+l_z) total edge length.

Third-octave band edges
f_l=f_c/2^{1/6},\qquad f_u=f_c\cdot2^{1/6}

50 Hz band: 44.5–56.1 Hz. Use the exact edges, not ±10 %.

Diffuse field on a wall
I_{\rm inc}=\frac{p^2}{4\rho c}=\frac{wc}{4}

A quarter of the plane-wave value: half the waves move away from the wall, the rest arrive with \overline{\cos\theta}=\tfrac12.

Energy balance
\frac{V}{\rho c^2}\frac{d(p^2)}{dt}=P_a-\frac{p^2A}{4\rho c}
p_s^2=\frac{4P_a\rho c}{A},\quad p^2(t)=p_s^2e^{-cAt/4V}

L_p\approx L_W+10\log(4/A).

Direct + reverberant
L_p=L_W+10\log\Big(\frac{Q}{4\pi r^2}+\frac{4(1-\bar\alpha)}{A}\Big)
r_{\rm rev}=\sqrt{\frac{A}{16\pi}}\approx0.14\sqrt A
Sabine (energy balance)
T=\frac{55.3V}{cA}\approx\frac{0.16V}{S\bar\alpha+4mV}

Good for \bar\alpha<0.3; wrongly gives T>0 for an anechoic room.

Eyring (ray reflections)
T=\frac{13.8\,l_m}{-c\ln(1-\bar\alpha)},\quad l_m=\frac{4V}{S}
T\approx\frac{0.16V}{-S\ln(1-\bar\alpha)}

Always shorter than Sabine; use it above \bar\alpha\approx0.3.

Measuring T
T_{30}=2(t_{-35}-t_{-5}),\quad T_{20}=3(t_{-25}-t_{-5})
\mathrm{EDT}=6(t_{-10}-t_0)

The ear judges reverberance from the early decay (EDT).

The traps
  • RMS, not peak. Room formulas (p^2=4P\rho c/A) use RMS p; Lecture 5's monopole formulas used peak amplitudes with a ½.
  • Odd index ⇒ node at the centre. Only all-even modes are heard in the middle of the room; all modes are maximal in the corners.
  • Degenerate means a permutation partner. In a cube (1,1,1) at 99 Hz is not degenerate.
  • L_eq of an intermittent source: average p^2 over time, not dB. On for a third of the time: −4.8 dB.
  • 0.16 = 55.3/c needs V in m³ and A in m². Don't mix c values within one calculation.
  • Absorption on one surface only (just the ceiling) makes the room non-diffuse, and the real T is far longer than Sabine predicts.
From the slides and the text

Measured natural frequencies come out slightly lower than calculated, because real walls give a little. Design small rooms with non-simple dimension ratios and calculate their modes below 100 Hz before building. Optimum T at 500–1000 Hz: cinema 0.4–1.0 s, lecture 0.8–1.2 s, symphony 1.5–2.2 s, organ 2.0–3.0 s. Air absorption (4mV; humid air absorbs less) is why big halls cannot stay reverberant at high frequency. Solution 6 prints 34.4 Hz for (1,0,0): a rounding slip, since c = 343 gives 34.3 Hz, as the sheet says.

Sound absorption and room acoustic design

Lecture 7 slides 1–53 · course text ch. 4, pp. 103–112, and §3.4.3–3.4.5 · Week 7 problems 1–2
The one ideaAn absorber puts friction where the air moves fastest: a porous layer a quarter wavelength out from the wall, or, when that is too far, a mass bouncing on the cavity air (a panel or the air plugs in holes).
Written on lecture day

This section was written from the slides and the course text on Tuesday 20 October 2026, before or during the lecture. It has not yet been checked against what was said in class (the in-class questions on slides 5 and 28, the hidden answer on slide 25). The Week 7 solutions are derived here, because the official solutions are not out yet. Every one matches the answer printed on the sheet.

The picture: three absorbers, three frequency ranges

TypeMechanismWorksKey formula
Porousviscous and thermal losses in the poresmid–high f; low limit set by thickness + air gapd\approx\lambda/4\Rightarrow f_{\min}\approx c/4d

Incident energy splits into absorbed \alpha, scattered (1-\alpha)\delta and specular (1-\alpha)(1-\delta). Slide 5: 70 dB incident on \alpha= 0.5, 0.9, 0.99 reflects 67, 60, 50 dB. The cavity spring \rho c^2/L is shared; only the mass per unit area changes.

Design budget (Sabine)

Absorption areas add; reverberation times do not. All absorption in the ceiling: T=0.16h/\alpha.

The traps
  • Normalise by ρc before the Paris formula: 0.53 turns into 0.0032 if you forget (slide 36).
  • \alpha_n\ne\alpha_r. A tube gives normal incidence; design needs random incidence.
  • λ/4 is measured from the wall, not from the front of the absorber.
  • Membrane: mass per unit area m=\rho_ph in kg/m², not the density.
  • Perforated panel: L is the cavity depth, l the panel thickness; P is a fraction (0.087), and \delta=0.8d uses the diameter.
  • SWR n is a pressure ratio. Given in dB? First n=10^{L/20}.
  • Sabine needs a diffuse field. All absorption on the ceiling, no scattering: the real T is longer than predicted.
Design notes from the slides and the text

Don't repaint mineral-wool tiles: the paint closes the pores. Porous material in a membrane cavity raises \alpha_{\max} and lowers the peak. DR Studio 1 ceiling resonators (slide 25; Ø65 mm neck, 6.75 and 16 L boxes) come out at about 98 and 64 Hz with \delta=0.8d (the slide hides the answer). Why put the source and the ear in a corner?

At a rigid wall the normal particle velocity is zero, so every mode has a pressure maximum there (\cos(n\pi x/L)=\pm1 at x=0,L). A corner is a pressure maximum in all three directions at once: a monopole source there excites every mode, and a microphone or ear there hears every mode. In the middle of the room every mode with an odd index has a node and disappears.

Reverberation time from the decay
T_{20}=3\,(t_{-25}-t_{-5}),\qquad T_{30}=2\,(t_{-35}-t_{-5})

Both extrapolate to 60 dB. Need about 35 dB SNR for T20 and 45 dB for T30 (the end point plus a 10 dB margin).

Script times

T_build_up ≥ the expected T60 (8–10 s empty); T_silence ≥ T60 at the lowest band; 5 decays × 3 positions. Check M = 12.5e-3*10 (GRAS 46AC, +20 dB) before the first run.

T20 vs T30, and what a healthy decay looks like

They agree if the decay is one straight line in dB. T30 > T20 means a double slope: slow axial and flutter paths between the hard parallel surfaces that never meet the absorbers, especially with all the absorption on one wall. T30 is invalid below about 45 dB SNR, because its −35 dB point sits in the noise floor and reads long; use T20 there (typically 125 Hz and 4 kHz) and say so in the report. A healthy decay has a clear stationary level before the cut, a straight decay over 20–30 dB, and a flat floor at least 10 dB below the range. It shows no clipping and no bumps from talking or doors. Some beating between modes at 125 Hz is normal.

Where do the diffusers work best?

With all the absorbers on one wall, sound bouncing between the side walls and between floor and ceiling never touches them. Put the diffusers on those hard parallel pairs, where the mirror paths hit, so they scatter the trapped energy towards the absorbing wall. Spread them out, and keep them away from the microphone positions. Expected trend: empty is longest with T20 ≈ T30. Absorbers cut mid and high frequencies but hardly 125 Hz, often with T30 > T20. Adding diffusers shortens T further and brings T20 and T30 together. Diffusers alone sit close to empty. The absorbers' α in the room is \Delta A/S_{\rm abs} with A=0.161V/T, so measure the panel area on the day.

Sound power in a reverberant room
L_W=L_p-14+10\log_{10}\frac{V}{1\,\mathrm{m^3}}-10\log_{10}\frac{T}{1\,\mathrm s}

From W=\langle p^2\rangle A/4\rho c and A=0.161V/T: 10\log(0.161/4)=-13.95 dB, and \rho c\approx400. Average p^2 over the positions before the log.

A-weighting and the total
L_{WA}=10\log_{10}\sum_i10^{(L_{W,i}+A_i)/10}

A_i = −16.1, −8.6, −3.2, 0, +1.2, +1.0 dB at 125 Hz – 4 kHz. Vacuum cleaners are typically 75–85 dB(A).

The traps
  • Below the Schroeder frequency (400–950 Hz here) a few modes dominate, so the 125 Hz T and L_p scatter from position to position. Three positions are few.
  • Average p^2, not dB, over positions.
  • Part C needs diffusers only, no absorbers, with the vacuum cleaner mid-room and away from the microphones. The reverberation distance is about 0.1 m, so the direct field is negligible beyond about 1 m.
  • The close mode pairs 52.2/54.9 and 65.3/67.4 Hz: sweep in about 0.2 Hz steps, and let the minima pattern decide.
