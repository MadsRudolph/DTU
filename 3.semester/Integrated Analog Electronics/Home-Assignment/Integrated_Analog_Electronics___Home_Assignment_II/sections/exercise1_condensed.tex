\section{Exercise 1: W/L Ratio in MOSFET Transistor}

Looking at the Shichman-Hodges model, the drain current is given by $I_D = \frac{1}{2} \mu_n C_{ox} \frac{W}{L} (V_{GS} - V_T)^2 (1 + \lambda V_{DS})$. The W/L ratio here basically works as a multiplier for transistor strength - W is the channel width (where current flows) and L is the distance carriers need to travel.

\textbf{What happens when we increase W:} The drain current scales linearly with W, so $I_D \propto W$. Meanwhile, transconductance goes up as $g_m \propto \sqrt{W}$, which gives better matching and lower on-resistance. But there's downsides - you get larger chip area, parasitic capacitances increase ($C_{gs}$ and $C_{gd}$ both scale with W), more power consumption, and the bandwidth drops because of those parasitics. Making W smaller reverses these effects.

\textbf{For increasing L:} The main benefit is higher output resistance $r_o$ (really useful for current sources), better matching since $\sigma(\Delta V_T) \propto 1/\sqrt{WL}$, and less channel-length modulation. However, $g_m$ decreases ($\propto 1/\sqrt{L}$), speed drops significantly - the unity-gain frequency actually scales as $1/L^2$ which is quite bad - and you use more area. Going shorter with L gives faster circuits and higher $g_m$ but at the cost of lower $r_o$, worse matching, and you start getting short-channel effects.

\textbf{Design considerations:} For practical design, large W/L works well for high-gain input stages, output drivers that need lots of current, and low-noise applications. Small W/L is better when you're trying to save power. In a typical 0.18$\mu$m process, we'd use something like L $\approx$ 1$\mu$m for current sources to get good $r_o$, maybe 20/0.5 for input pairs to maximize $g_m$, and 90/0.5 for outputs when we need current drive capability.
