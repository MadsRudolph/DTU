\section{Exercise 4: Bandgap Voltage Reference}

The basic idea behind a bandgap reference is to add together voltages with opposite temperature coefficients to get something temperature-independent. $V_{BE}$ has CTAT behavior (it drops about 2 mV/K with temperature) while $\Delta V_{BE} = V_T\ln(M) = (kT/q)\ln(M)$ is PTAT, where M is the current density ratio between the BJTs. When you sum them as $V_{REF} = V_{BE} + G\cdot\Delta V_{BE}$, you get around 1.25V which happens to be silicon's bandgap energy. The gain $G = R_2/R_1$ is picked so that $\partial V_{REF}/\partial T = 0$.

How it works: an op-amp forces equal voltages at its inputs, which develops $\Delta V_{BE}$ across a resistor and creates a PTAT current. In CMOS you use parasitic substrate PNP transistors for this. You need a startup circuit too, otherwise the circuit can get stuck in a zero-current state.

\textbf{Advantages:} The temperature coefficient is really low (10-20 ppm/°C), you get good PSRR, and since it's based on physical constants it doesn't depend much on the process. It can be fully integrated which makes it useful for ADC/DAC references, LDO regulators, and temperature sensors.

\textbf{Disadvantages:} You need at least 1.5V supply for $V_{REF}$ plus some headroom. There are still some second-order temperature effects, the startup circuit adds complexity, and process variation can be ±20-30\% so you often need trimming. Noise from the resistors and op-amp is also an issue. If you need to work below 1.2V you need special topologies.
