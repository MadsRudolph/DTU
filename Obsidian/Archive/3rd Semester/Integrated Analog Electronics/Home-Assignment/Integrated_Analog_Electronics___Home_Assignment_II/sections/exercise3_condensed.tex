\section{Exercise 3: Second-Order Feedback System}

Second-order systems have the characteristic equation $s^2 + s\omega_0/Q + \omega_0^2$ where $\omega_0 = \sqrt{(1+L_0)\omega_{p1}\omega_{p2}}$ is the natural frequency and $Q = \omega_0/(\omega_{p1}+\omega_{p2})$ is the quality factor. How the system responds depends on the damping ratio $\zeta = 1/(2Q)$.

\textbf{Overdamped ($\zeta > 1$):} Here you have widely separated real poles ($\omega_{p2} \gg \omega_t$) which gives maximum stability with no overshoot. The downside is you sacrifice bandwidth and settling time. This is what you'd go for if stability matters more than speed.

\textbf{Critically damped ($\zeta = 1$):} With repeated real poles ($\omega_{p2} \approx 4\omega_t$) you theoretically get the fastest response without overshoot. But in practice this is hard to achieve since component tolerances will always push the system toward being either over- or underdamped.

\textbf{Underdamped ($0 < \zeta < 1$):} Complex conjugate poles ($\omega_{p2} < 4\omega_t$) give you higher bandwidth and faster response, but you'll get overshoot and ringing. When $Q > 0.707$, you also get frequency peaking in the magnitude response.

\textbf{Pole placement strategy:} Using dominant-pole compensation creates wide pole separation, which makes the system behave like a first-order one and keeps it stable. When poles are close together they interact and degrade the phase margin. Miller compensation does pole splitting - moves $p_1$ lower and $p_2$ higher. You generally want phase margin $\geq 60^\circ$ for well-damped response. Each $10^\circ$ drop in phase margin roughly doubles the overshoot. If you hit exactly $65.5^\circ$ (which corresponds to $\omega_{p2} = 2\omega_t$) you get a Butterworth response with maximally flat frequency response.
