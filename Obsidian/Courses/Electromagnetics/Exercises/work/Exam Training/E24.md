## Q1 — Wave Frequency

![[Q1.png|400]]

> The amplitude of a wave is shown as a function of time in the figure above.  
> **What is the frequency of the wave?**

### Theory  
The **period** $T$ is the time it takes for one full cycle. From the graph you read off the distance between two identical points on the waveform (e.g. peak–to–peak).  

The **frequency** is then  
$$
f = \frac{1}{T}.
$$  

If, for example, one period corresponds to $\Delta t = 4\ \mathrm{ms}$, then  
$$
f = \frac{1}{4\cdot10^{-3}} = 250\ \text{Hz}.
$$  

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] 500 Hz  
- [x] 250 Hz  
- [ ] 125 Hz  
- [ ] 1 kHz

## Q2 — Phase Velocity from Two Measurements

![[Q2.png|400]]

> The amplitude of a wave is measured as a function of time at **two different positions** distanced **1 m apart**. The measurements are shown in the figure above. Assume the distance is **less than one wavelength**.  
> **What is the phase velocity of the wave?**

### Theory  
The **phase velocity** is  
$$
v_p = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t_\phi},
$$  
where $\Delta x$ is the distance between two observation points and $\Delta t_\phi$ is the time shift between them for **the same phase point** (e.g. crest→crest).  

Procedure:  

1. From the plots, find the time shift $\Delta t_\phi$ between identical features of the signals (same amplitude & slope).  
2. Use the given separation $\Delta x = 1\ \text{m}$.  
3. Compute $v_p$ with the formula above and convert to the units given (e.g. km/s or m/s).  

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] 4 km/s  
- [ ] 1 km/s  
- [ ] 2 km/s  
- [ ] 10 m/s

## Q3 — Electrical Length from Phase Delay


> A signal is transmitted through a **matched transmission line (TL)**. A phase delay of  
> $$
 \Delta\phi = \frac{\pi}{5}
 $$  
> is measured between the input and output of the TL.  
> **What is the electrical length $\ell/\lambda$ of the TL?**

### Theory  
For a lossless TL,
$$
\beta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}, \qquad \theta = \beta\ell,
$$  
where $\theta$ is the **phase shift** across length $\ell$.  

We are given $\theta = \Delta\phi$ so  
$$
\Delta\phi = \beta \ell = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,\ell
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\frac{\ell}{\lambda} = \frac{\Delta\phi}{2\pi}.
$$  

Insert $\Delta\phi = \pi/5$ to get the numerical value.

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] 0.4  
- [ ] 0.2  
- [ ] 0.1  
- [ ] 0.05

## Q4 — Coaxial Cable Parameter Comparison

> Which wave parameter is **not** the same for two coaxial cables consisting of the **same materials**, **same outer conductor radius**, but **different inner conductor radius**?

### Theory  
For a coaxial line, the **characteristic impedance** is  
$$
Z_0 = \frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}} \ln\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right),
$$  
where $a$ is the inner conductor radius and $b$ is the outer conductor radius.  

- Changing $a$ (inner radius) **changes $\ln(b/a)$**, hence changes $Z_0$.  
- The **phase velocity**
  $$
  v_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}}
  $$
  depends only on material properties, not geometry.  
- The **propagation constant** $\gamma = \alpha + j\beta$ and **phase constant** $\beta$ also depend on material parameters and frequency, not on $a$ if loss is negligible.  

Thus only $Z_0$ changes when you change the inner conductor radius while keeping material and $b$ fixed.

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] The characteristic impedance  
- [ ] The propagation constant  
- [ ] The phase constant  
- [ ] The phase velocity

## Q7 — VSWR of a Short-Circuited Lossless TL

> A lossless TL is terminated with a **short-circuit**.  
> **What is the voltage standing wave ratio, VSWR, of the total wave in the TL?**

### Theory  
For a short-circuit load, $Z_L = 0$ so

$$
\Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}
      = \frac{-Z_0}{Z_0} = -1,
$$
so $|\Gamma| = 1$.  

The VSWR is
$$
\text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + |\Gamma|}{1 - |\Gamma|}.
$$
If $|\Gamma| = 1$, denominator is zero and VSWR $\to \infty$. This corresponds to **complete reflection** and perfectly formed standing waves.

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] -1  
- [ ] 0  
- [ ] 1  
- [ ] ∞

   $$
   |\Gamma| = 10^{\frac{-8}{20}}
   $$

3. Once the complex $\Gamma$ at the **load** is known,
   $$
   Z_L = Z_0 \frac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma}.

Graphically, this is often done on a Smith chart using the measured $|\Gamma|$ and the distance to a current maximum.

## Q9 — Input Impedance for ℓ = λ

![[Q9.png|500]]

> What is the **input impedance at ref. plane A** of the TL circuit shown above?  
> The line has length $\ell = \lambda$, characteristic impedance $Z_0 = 50\ \Omega$, and load impedance $Z_L = 35\ \Omega$.

### Theory  
For a **lossless TL**, the input impedance is periodic in length with period $\lambda$:

$$
Z_{\text{in}}(\ell) = Z_0
\frac{Z_L + j Z_0 \tan(\beta\ell)}{Z_0 + j Z_L \tan(\beta\ell)}.
$$  

If $\ell = \lambda$, then $\beta\ell = 2\pi$ and $\tan(2\pi) = 0$, giving
$$
Z_{\text{in}}(\lambda) = Z_0\frac{Z_L}{Z_0} = Z_L.
$$  

So a line of one wavelength is **impedance repeating**: the input sees exactly the load impedance.

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] 35 Ω  
- [ ] 24.5 Ω  
- [ ] 71.4 Ω  
- [ ] 50 Ω

## Q10 — Two Quarter-Wave Lines in Parallel

![[Q10.png|500]]

> What is the **input impedance** of a **parallel connection of two identical quarter-wave TLs** terminated with identical loads, as shown above?  
> The characteristic impedance of the TLs is denoted $Z_0$ and the load impedance is $Z_L$.

### Theory  

Each **quarter-wave ($\lambda/4$) TL** acts as an impedance inverter:
$$
Z_{\text{in, single}} = \frac{Z_0^2}{Z_L}.
$$  

With **two identical branches in parallel**, the equivalent impedance is
$$
Z_{\text{eq}} = \left( \frac{1}{Z_{\text{in, single}}}
                 + \frac{1}{Z_{\text{in, single}}} \right)^{-1}
             = \frac{Z_{\text{in, single}}}{2}
             = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Z_0^2}{Z_L}.
$$  

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] $Z_0^2 / Z_L$  
- [ ] $Z_L/2$  
- [ ] $Z_0^2 / (2 Z_L)$  
- [ ] $Z_L$

## Q11 — Stub Inductance Design

> Design a TL stub functioning as an **inductor** with an equivalent inductance of **20 nH**.  
> The TL stub has an **open termination** and the operating frequency is **2 GHz**.  
> The line has characteristic impedance $Z_0 = 75\ \Omega$ and phase velocity $u_p = 0.66 c_0$.  
> **What is the shortest possible length of the stub?**

### Theory  

For an **open-circuited stub**, the input impedance is
$$
Z_{\text{stub}} = -j Z_0 \cot(\beta\ell).
$$  

We want this to behave like an **inductive reactance**
$$
Z_{\text{eq}} = j\omega L.
$$  

So equate:
$$
-j Z_0 \cot(\beta\ell) = j\omega L
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\cot(\beta\ell) = -\frac{\omega L}{Z_0}.
$$  

Given
$$
\beta = \frac{\omega}{u_p} = \frac{2\pi f}{u_p},
$$
solve for $\ell$ in
$$
\beta\ell = \cot^{-1}\!\left(-\frac{\omega L}{Z_0}\right),
$$
and choose the **smallest positive** $\ell$ that satisfies this (modulo $\pi$).

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] 68.0 mm  
- [ ] 20.16 mm  
- [ ] 32.4 mm  
- [ ] 44.9 mm

## Q15 — Single-Stub Tuner (Shortest Lengths)

![[Q15_img1.png|500]]

> A single-stub tuner is inserted between a TL and a load as shown above.  
> The load impedance is $Z_L = (110 - j35)\ \Omega$ and the stub termination is a **short-circuit**.  
> **Calculate the shortest possible lengths** $d$ and $\ell$ of the TLs for the single-stub tuner, given in wavelengths.

### Theory  

Goal: transform the load admittance to the **matched conductance** $g = 1$ and cancel susceptance using the stub.

Steps (Smith chart or algebra):

1. Normalize $Z_L$ to $Z_0$ and find corresponding point on Smith chart.  
2. Move from the load toward the generator by distance $d$ along the **constant SWR circle** until the **conductance** $g(d) = 1$ (real part of admittance equals 1).  
3. At that point, there remains some susceptance $b(d)$.  
4. The short-circuited stub is placed in **parallel**, contributing susceptance $b_s$:
   $$
   y_{\text{total}} = 1 + j[b(d) + b_s] = 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad b_s = -b(d).
   $$  
5. Map the required $b_s$ to a shorted stub length $\ell$ using the **admittance** Smith chart or
   $$
   Y_{\text{stub}} = j B = j \frac{1}{Z_0}\tan(\beta\ell) \quad\text{(shorted)}.
   $$

- [ ] $d = 0.121\lambda$ and $\ell = 0.3\lambda$  
- [ ] $d = 0.0981\lambda$ and $\ell = 0.405\lambda$

## Q17 — Required Conductor Thickness (Shielding)

> A conductor with conductivity $\sigma = 58\ \text{MS/m}$ and relative permeability $\mu_r = 3$ is used for **electromagnetic shielding** in the frequency range from **10 MHz to 10 GHz**.  
> **What is the requirement for the thickness** of the conductor, $t_c$, to ensure electromagnetic shielding at all frequencies according to the rule of thumb?

### Theory  

The **skin depth** is
$$
\delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}},
\quad \mu = \mu_0 \mu_r.
$$  

For good shielding, a common rule of thumb is
$$
t_c \ge 5\delta_{\max},
$$
where $\delta_{\max}$ is the **largest** skin depth in the frequency range, which occurs at the **lowest frequency** (10 MHz).  

Steps:

1. Compute $\omega_{\min} = 2\pi (10\ \text{MHz})$.  
2. Evaluate $\delta$ at that frequency.  
3. Multiply by 5 and convert to $\mu$m.  

### Answer Choices  
- [x] $t_c > 60.3\ \mu\text{m}$  
- [ ] $t_c > 104\ \mu\text{m}$  
- [ ] $t_c > 12.1\ \mu\text{m}$

## Q19 — Phasor Amplitude of the Electric Field

> A plane wave is propagating in air. The electric field is given by  
> $$
\vec{E}(y,t) =
\begin{pmatrix}
5\cos(\omega t + 10y)\\[2pt]
0\\[2pt]
-15\sin(\omega t + 10y)
\end{pmatrix}
\ \frac{\text{V}}{\text{m}}.
$$  
> What is the **phasor amplitude** $\tilde{\vec E}$?

### Theory  

Use the correct decomposition:
$$
\vec E(\vec r,t)
= \vec E_{0,r}\cos(\omega t - \vec\beta\cdot\vec r)
- \vec E_{0,i}\sin(\omega t - \vec\beta\cdot\vec r),
$$
which corresponds to the phasor
$$
\tilde{\vec E} = \vec E_{0,r} + j\,\vec E_{0,i}.
$$

The field depends on $\omega t + 10y = \omega t - (-10y)$,  
so $\vec\beta\cdot\vec r = -10y$.

Now read off the coefficients:

- From the $\cos(\cdot)$ term:
  $$
  \vec E_{0,r} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
  5\\[2pt]
  0\\[2pt]
  0
  \end{pmatrix}.
  $$

- From the $-\sin(\cdot)$ term:
  $$
  -E_{0,i,z}\sin(\cdot) = -15\sin(\cdot)
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  E_{0,i,z} = 15.
  $$
  So
  $$
  \vec E_{0,i} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
  0\\[2pt]
  0\\[2pt]
  15
  \end{pmatrix}.
  $$

Therefore the phasor amplitude is:
$$
\tilde{\vec E}
= \vec E_{0,r} + j\vec E_{0,i}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
5\\[2pt]
0\\[2pt]
j15
\end{pmatrix}
\frac{\text{V}}{\text{m}}.
$$

### Answer choices  

- [ ] $\displaystyle \begin{pmatrix}-j5\\[2pt]0\\[2pt]-15\end{pmatrix}\ \frac{\text{V}}{\text{m}}$  
- [ ] $\displaystyle \begin{pmatrix}5\\[2pt]0\\[2pt]15\end{pmatrix}\ \frac{\text{V}}{\text{m}}$  
- [ ] $\displaystyle \begin{pmatrix}5\\[2pt]0\\[2pt]-j15\end{pmatrix}\ \frac{\text{V}}{\text{m}}$  
- [x] $\displaystyle \begin{pmatrix}5\\[2pt]0\\[2pt]j15\end{pmatrix}\ \frac{\text{V}}{\text{m}}$

## Q22 — Axial Ratio of the Wave

> For the wave in Q21, **what is the axial ratio** of its polarization ellipse?

### Theory  

The **axial ratio (AR)** is
$$
\text{AR} = \frac{\text{major axis}}{\text{minor axis}}.
$$  

Special cases:

- **Linear** polarization: minor axis $= 0$ ⇒ $\text{AR} = \infty$.  
- **Circular** polarization: major = minor ⇒ $\text{AR} = 1$.  
- **Elliptical** (neither linear nor circular) has finite AR $> 1$.  

Since we found equal magnitudes and $\pm 90^\circ$ phase difference, it is **circular** ⇒ $\text{AR} = 1$.

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] 100  
- [ ] 1  
- [ ] 10  
- [ ] ∞

## Q24 — Reflection from PEC


> A plane wave is **normally incident** on a plane metallic medium, which is considered to be a **perfect electric conductor (PEC)**.  
> **What is the reflection coefficient $\Gamma$ of the reflected wave?**

### Theory  

At a PEC boundary:

- The **tangential electric field must be zero** at the surface.  
- The incident and reflected fields must cancel at the boundary:
  $$
  E_{\text{inc}} + E_{\text{refl}} = 0 \Rightarrow E_{\text{refl}} = -E_{\text{inc}}.
  $$  

Thus
$$
\Gamma = \frac{E_{\text{refl}}}{E_{\text{inc}}} = -1.
$$  

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] $\Gamma = 0$  
- [ ] $\Gamma = 1$  
- [ ] $\Gamma = -1$  
- [ ] $\Gamma = 2$

## Q30 — Avoiding Dielectric Breakdown

> How should the **surface of a conductor** be shaped to avoid **dielectric breakdown**?

### Theory  

Electric field near a conductor surface is stronger where the curvature is **high** (sharp points). For a given total charge, the surface charge density $\sigma$ concentrates at sharp corners, leading to high local $E$ and possible breakdown of surrounding insulator (air, dielectric).  

To reduce breakdown risk:

- Make the surface **smooth**.  
- Use **large radius of curvature** (flat / rounded shapes).

## Q33 — Magnetic Flux Density at Center of Current-Carrying Wire

> What is the magnitude of the magnetic flux density $\vec{B}$ at the **center** of an infinitely long wire of radius $2\ \text{mm}$ carrying a current of $2\ \text{A}$?

### Theory  

For a long cylindrical conductor with **uniform current density**:

- Outside ($r \ge a$): $B(r) = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$.  
- Inside ($r < a$): $B(r) = \dfrac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi a^2}$ (from Ampère’s law with only the enclosed fraction of current).  

At the **center** ($r = 0$),
$$
B(0) = 0.
$$  

So despite the current, the symmetry forces the magnetic field to vanish exactly at the center.

## Q35 — Magnetic Field of an Infinite Current Sheet

> We consider an **infinite current sheet** with air above and below it.  
> The sheet lies in the **$xy$-plane** and has a surface current density  
> $$
 \vec{J}_S = \hat{x} J_0,
 $$
> where $J_0$ [A/m] is a constant.  
> **What is the correct expression for the magnetic flux density above the current sheet, i.e. for positive $z$-coordinates ($z > 0$)?**

### Theory  

For an infinite current sheet with surface current $\vec{J}_s$, Ampère’s law gives a **discontinuity** in the tangential magnetic field across the sheet:

$$
\hat{n} \times (\vec{H}_2 - \vec{H}_1) = \vec{J}_s,
$$
where $\hat{n}$ is the unit normal (here $\hat{z}$).  

Symmetry implies:

- Fields depend only on $z$.  
- $\vec{H}$ is parallel to the sheet and perpendicular to $\vec{J}_s$; here $\vec{H}$ is along $\pm\hat{y}$.  

Solving gives magnitude
$$
H = \frac{J_0}{2}
$$
on each side, with directions opposite above and below the sheet.  

Then
$$
\vec{B} = \mu_0 \vec{H}.
$$  

Above the sheet ($z>0$), the direction follows from right-hand rule.

### Answer Choices  
- [ ] $\hat{z}\,\dfrac{\mu_0 J_0}{2}$  
- [ ] $\hat{y}\,\dfrac{\mu_0 J_0}{2}$  
- [ ] $-\hat{y}\,\dfrac{\mu_0 J_0}{2}$  
- [ ] $-\hat{z}\,\dfrac{\mu_0 J_0}{2}$
